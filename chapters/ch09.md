# Invariant densities, intermittency, and the baker map

A deterministic device often loses usable information about its starting state before losing predictability about its statistics. If a normalized meter follows the logistic recurrence at parameter four, infinitesimal perturbations typically grow under the tangent dynamics. Yet a long run under a typical start has a definite fraction of readings near each endpoint. To calculate that fraction, evolve a distribution of starting states instead of a single point. This chapter develops the distribution operator, uses it to distinguish a frequent slow passage from weak expansion, and then restores invertibility by adding a coordinate that remembers the discarded bits.

## Evolve the probability law by transporting mass [L19 00:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3klI2Jv3brU&t=56s)

An ensemble assigns a probability measure to the starting state. For a measurable map $f$ on an interval $I$, let $\mu_n$ be the distribution of the state after $n$ steps. The next-state probability of a set $B$ equals the current probability of its preimage, $\mu_{n+1}(B)=\mu_n(f^{-1}B)$. A probability measure $\mu$ is **invariant** when this equation returns $\mu$ itself. An invariant measure need not be a density. A point mass at a fixed point is invariant, as is the uniform measure on a periodic orbit under cyclic permutation.

The continuous-time counterpart for a smooth flow $\dot z=F(z)$ is the transport equation $\partial_t\rho=-\nabla\cdot(\rho F)$ for a probability density $\rho$ with respect to volume. For a smooth test function $a$, the chain rule applied to the trajectory average gives $d\int a\rho\,dz/dt=\int \nabla a\cdot F\,\rho\,dz$. Integration by parts with compact support, or with zero normal flux on the boundary, gives the transport equation and its sign. If the flow is Hamiltonian in canonical coordinates then $\nabla\cdot F=0$ and the density is constant along trajectories. Any nonnegative function of the Hamiltonian with finite, positive phase-space integral can be normalized to a stationary probability density on an invariant phase space. A thermal model may select a canonical density proportional to $e^{-H/(k_B T)}$, where $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T$ is absolute temperature. That choice adds an equilibrium assumption beyond stationarity.

## Invert each branch to obtain the transfer operator [L19 15:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3klI2Jv3brU&t=900s)

Suppose the map has finitely many monotone $C^1$ branches with differentiable inverses on the interiors of their images. If $\mu_n$ has density $\rho_n$ with respect to interval length, then the change of variables $x=f(u)$ on each branch converts

$$
\int_I a(x)\rho_{n+1}(x)\,dx
=\int_I a(f(u))\rho_n(u)\,du
$$

into the Perron–Frobenius equation

$$
\rho_{n+1}(x)=(P_f\rho_n)(x)
=\sum_{u:\,f(u)=x}\frac{\rho_n(u)}{|f'(u)|}
$$

for almost every $x$. Only branches whose images contain $x$ contribute. Each preimage brings mass proportional to the length of its own inverse interval. A rapidly expanding branch compresses its contribution per unit length, and a slow branch contributes more density for the same incoming density. The absolute value accounts for orientation reversal. The formula can fail pointwise at critical values, while the change-of-variables identity still specifies the measure.

Taking $a=1$ proves that $P_f$ preserves total mass. A stationary density must satisfy $\rho=P_f\rho$, $\rho\ge0$, and $\int_I\rho=1$. For the doubling map $D$ of [Chapter 8](ch08.html),

$$
(P_D\rho)(x)=\frac12\rho(x/2)+\frac12\rho((x+1)/2).
$$

The constant density $\rho=1$ gives each interval probability equal to its length. This fixed-point calculation establishes invariance. For convergence of time averages under this law, one also needs ergodicity. With a uniform binary expansion, dyadic-cylinder events depend on finitely many bits. The initial bits and the bits entering an event after a sufficiently large shift are independent, so the mixing identity holds exactly for these events beyond their overlap length. Approximation of measurable sets by finite unions of dyadic intervals extends it to the Lebesgue measure on the interval. The mixing claim does not cover every invariant measure of doubling. A point mass at zero supplies an immediate different law. [L19 28:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3klI2Jv3brU&t=1707s)

## Calculate the logistic density at parameter four [L19 48:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3klI2Jv3brU&t=2894s)

Take $f(x)=4x(1-x)$ and define $h(u)=\sin^2(\pi u)$ on $u\in[0,1)$. Then

$$
f(h(u))=4\sin^2(\pi u)\cos^2(\pi u)=\sin^2(2\pi u)=h(D(u)).
$$

The identity says that applying $h$ after doubling gives the same output as applying the logistic map after $h$. Because $h(u)=h(1-u)$, this is a **semiconjugacy** from doubling to logistic evolution, a map intertwining the dynamics without being one-to-one. The pushforward of the uniform law by $h$ is invariant: the doubling step preserves the original law before the same $h$ is applied. For a point $x\in(0,1)$ there are two symmetric preimages under $h$, and each has $|h'(u)|=2\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)}$. The invariant density and its cumulative distribution are consequently

$$
\rho_*(x)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)}},\qquad
\mu_*([0,x])=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\sqrt{x}.
$$

Unlike an atom, each endpoint singularity is integrable. For example, the fraction in $[0,1/4]$ under this law is $1/3$, higher than its length $1/4$. The distribution is symmetric about $1/2$, so the corresponding upper quarter has the same mass. The overdensity near the edges follows from the small derivative of the observation function $h$, which maps equal lengths of the doubling coordinate to unequal lengths of the logistic coordinate.

Ergodicity of the doubling law passes to the logistic law: an $f$-invariant event pulls back through $h$ to a $D$-invariant event, with the same probability. The Lyapunov exponent of [Chapter 8](ch08.html) is therefore the average of $\log|f'|$ under $\rho_*$ for $\mu_*$-almost every point, provided the logarithm is integrable. In the $u$ coordinate, differentiate the semiconjugacy wherever both branches are regular. Then

$$
\log|f'(h(u))|
=\log 2+\log|h'(D(u))|-\log|h'(u)|.
$$

The singularities of $\log|h'|$ are logarithmic and integrable. Its two contributions have equal integrals because the $u$ measure is invariant. Hence $\lambda=\log 2$ for almost every $x$ under $\mu_*$. A special orbit such as the fixed point $x=0$ has multiplier $4$ and exponent $\log 4$. A fixed density and one exceptional orbit can support different precise statements about the same map. [L19 55:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3klI2Jv3brU&t=3313s)

## A second exact substitution gives concentration at a point [L19 65:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3klI2Jv3brU&t=3907s)

At logistic parameter $2$, set $u_n=1-2x_n$. Then $1-4x_n(1-x_n)=(1-2x_n)^2$, so $u_{n+1}=u_n^2$ and

$$
x_n=\frac{1-(1-2x_0)^{2^n}}2 .
$$

An interior start has $|1-2x_0|<1$ and converges to $1/2$. For $x_0=0$ or $1$ the orbit stays at or reaches the fixed endpoint $0$. The attracting invariant law for an ensemble of interior starts is therefore a point mass at $1/2$, in the sense that averages of continuous observables converge to their value there. Differentiating the exact iterate gives $(f^n)'(x_0)=2^n(1-2x_0)^{2^n-1}$. For interior $x_0\ne1/2$, the log derivative divided by $n$ tends to $-\infty$ because $\log|1-2x_0|<0$ and $(2^n-1)/n$ diverges. At $x_0=1/2$ the product vanishes after one step. Comparing this result with parameter $4$ gives two exactly solvable laws with very different dynamics and measures.

## Resolve a slow channel near a tangent bifurcation [L20 13:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wqnQp5C4gTk&t=784s)

An intermittent record alternates long episodes of apparently regular behavior with shorter irregular bursts. A local map near a tangent bifurcation can account for a slow, regular episode. Shift the tangency to zero and consider

$$
x_{n+1}=x_n+\varepsilon+x_n^2
$$

inside a small channel $|x|\le b$, with $b>0$ fixed. At $\varepsilon<0$ there are local fixed points $\pm\sqrt{-\varepsilon}$, provided both are in the channel. The one on the left attracts for small $|\varepsilon|$ because its derivative is $1-2\sqrt{-\varepsilon}$. At $\varepsilon=0$ they meet. For $\varepsilon>0$ each step advances to the right, but the advancement is small near zero. A continuous passage-time approximation replaces the discrete increment with $dx/dn=\varepsilon+x^2$. Integrating across the channel gives

$$
N_{\rm approx}
=\int_{-b}^{b}\frac{dx}{\varepsilon+x^2}
=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\arctan\!\frac{b}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}.
$$

For fixed $b$ and $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, this approximation grows as $\pi/\sqrt{\varepsilon}$. Its mechanism is an $O(\sqrt\varepsilon)$ central length traversed in per-step increments of order $\varepsilon$. The scaling concerns a channel passage once a trajectory enters it from the left. To observe repeated laminar episodes, the global map must return states to the channel. Passage time alone gives neither the return probability nor a theorem for the mean laminar length under arbitrary reinjection. Pomeau and Manneville's original paper reports widely separated bursts at transitions from periodic to chaotic behavior. [Pomeau and Manneville, Intermittent Transition to Turbulence in Dissipative Dynamical Systems](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01197757)

## An invariant law can overweight slow motion [L20 24:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wqnQp5C4gTk&t=1478s)

The full cusp map $C(x)=1-2\sqrt{|x|}$ sends $[-1,1]$ onto itself on each side of zero. Its slope at $-1$ is $+1$ and for $x=-1+u$ with small $u>0$,

$$
C(-1+u)=-1+u+\frac{u^2}{4}+O(u^3).
$$

A trajectory injected near $-1$ advances slowly even though each regular interior branch has absolute derivative $|x|^{-1/2}>1$. For a target $x$ the two preimages are $\pm s$ with $s=(1-x)^2/4$, each contributing inverse slope $(1-x)/2$. Try $\rho(x)=(1-x)/2$. Then $\rho(s)+\rho(-s)=1$, giving $P_C\rho=\rho$ and a normalized density. The residence probability in the leftmost quarter of the domain, $[-1,-1/2]$, is $7/16$, while uniform length assigns $1/4$. This exact calculation establishes how the full branch geometry changes the weight of the slow region.

The mean log stretch under this density is $1/2$ per step. Indeed, $\log|C'(x)|=-\tfrac12\log|x|$, and the odd part of $\rho(x)$ integrates to zero against this even function. The remaining integral reduces to $-\tfrac12\int_0^1\log x\,dx=1/2$. An invariant-ensemble mean is also a time-average exponent for almost every orbit if the stated measure is ergodic. Invariance alone here establishes the ensemble calculation. In particular the marginal fixed point at $-1$ separately supports a point mass with zero stretch.

Altering one branch can maintain local slow points while changing residence statistics. Keep $C$ on the negative branch and take $2\sqrt x-1$ on the positive branch. The inverse derivative weights become $(1-x)/2$ and $(1+x)/2$, whose sum is one. Consequently this altered map has invariant density $1/2$ across $[-1,1]$. Equal invariant occupancy at two sets of equal length is a statement about their one-time marginal law. By itself it does not establish the statistics of residence times or correlations. [L20 43:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wqnQp5C4gTk&t=2594s)

One can also construct a map for a specified density. If a target probability density has a continuous strictly increasing cumulative distribution $F:I\to[0,1]$, then $F(X)$ is uniform when $X$ has the target law. Define a new evolution $x\mapsto F^{-1}(D(F(x)))$, using a half-open convention at the top endpoint. Since doubling leaves the uniform law invariant, the target density is invariant for the constructed map. This scheme selects an evolution with the requested invariant law but does not characterize every evolution sharing that law. [L20 51:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wqnQp5C4gTk&t=3114s)

## Recover the lost past by adding a coordinate [L20 54:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wqnQp5C4gTk&t=3252s)

On the half-open unit square define the baker map $B$ with branch bit $j=\lfloor2x\rfloor$ by

$$
B(x,y)=\left(2x-j,\frac{y+j}{2}\right).
$$

Each vertical half is stretched horizontally and compressed vertically to become a horizontal half. The branch Jacobian $\operatorname{diag}(2,1/2)$ has determinant one, and the branch images partition the square. The map preserves area and is invertible. From $(u,v)=B(x,y)$ recover $j=\lfloor2v\rfloor$, then $x=(u+j)/2$ and $y=2v-j$. The horizontal coordinate alone is the doubling map and loses the leading bit. The vertical coordinate keeps it as its new leading bit. This is a concrete explanation of how projection can erase information even when the complete dynamics retain it.

An orbit avoiding partition boundaries has Lyapunov exponents $+\log2$ horizontally and $-\log2$ vertically, because its $n$-step tangent matrix is $\operatorname{diag}(2^n,2^{-n})$. Their sum is zero, consistent with area preservation. Lebesgue area also gives independent equiprobable bits for the two coordinates. Reading the $y$ digits as the past in reverse chronological order and $x$ digits as the future makes one application of $B$ a shift of the dividing index in a doubly infinite binary sequence. Cylinder events, which depend on finitely many specified bits, become independent once their index sets separate under the shift. Approximation by cylinders gives mixing for area measure. Expansion and mixing were established by different calculations.

Geometrically the image of the left half after $n$ steps consists of horizontal strips of total area $1/2$. In a fixed coarse window their widths shrink as $n$ grows, and their intersection area approaches half the window's area. Discarding the strip arrangement by averaging is a change in observation scale. The exact baker map remains reversible at every step. [L20 61:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wqnQp5C4gTk&t=3718s)

## Exercises

1. **Verify the mass factor.** For the full tent $T_1$, use the two inverses to show that the uniform density is invariant.
2. **Separate an invariant law from a point orbit.** At logistic parameter four, compute the invariant-law probability of $[1/4,3/4]$ and compare it with the fraction of time spent there by the fixed point $3/4$.
3. **Change the observation.** Apply the proof using $h(u)=\sin^2(\pi u)$ to explain why the density singularities at the endpoints have exponent $-1/2$.
4. **Rescale a laminar channel.** In the continuous approximation replace $x^2$ by $a x^2$ with $a>0$. Find the leading full-channel time for fixed $b$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$.
5. **Recover exact coordinates.** Apply the baker map to $(5/8,1/4)$ twice. Use the inverse formula to reconstruct the initial point.
6. **Measure a dyed region.** For $A=\{x<1/2\}$ and $W=\{0\le y<1/4\}$ in the unit square, compute the area of $B^n(A)\cap W$ at $n=1,2,3$. Explain why the value stays constant thereafter.

<summary>Worked solutions</summary>

1. A target $y\in(0,1)$ has preimages $y/2$ and $1-y/2$, both with absolute slope $2$. The transfer operator of the unit density gives $1/2+1/2=1$. Values at the endpoints do not affect the absolutely continuous law.
2. The lower tail up to $1/4$ has probability $1/3$, and by symmetry the upper tail from $3/4$ has the same probability. The central interval therefore has mass $1/3$. The map sends $3/4$ to itself, so that initial point spends fraction one in the closed central interval. It is an exceptional initial condition relative to the continuous law.
3. Near $u=0$, $x=h(u)\sim\pi^2u^2$, so the inverse varies as $\sqrt x/\pi$ and its derivative as $1/(2\pi\sqrt x)$. A second preimage near $u=1$ gives the same contribution. Near $x=1$, $u$ is close to $1/2$ and $1-x\sim\pi^2(u-1/2)^2$, giving the matching square-root singularity.
4. Substitute $v=x\sqrt{a/\varepsilon}$ to obtain $N_{\rm approx}=2\arctan(b\sqrt{a/\varepsilon})/\sqrt{a\varepsilon}$. The arctangent tends to $\pi/2$, so the leading term is $\pi/\sqrt{a\varepsilon}$.
5. The first bit is one and the first image is $(1/4,5/8)$. The next bit is zero and the next image is $(1/2,5/16)$. From the latter, $j=\lfloor 2(5/16)\rfloor=0$, giving $(1/4,5/8)$. Now $j=\lfloor 2(5/8)\rfloor=1$, giving $(5/8,1/4)$.
6. The initial leading $x$ bit is zero. After $n$ shifts it becomes the $n$th $y$ bit, so $B^n(A)$ is the set where that bit vanishes. At $n=1$ and $n=2$, $W$ forces the relevant bit to zero and all of $W$ belongs to the intersection, with area $1/4$. At $n=3$ and all higher $n$, the fixed bit is independent of the two leading bits defining $W$. The intersection then has half the area of $W$, or $1/8$.
