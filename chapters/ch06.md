# The Poincaré index and qualitative diagnostics

Suppose a mechanical system with two possible resting configurations loses energy to friction. A phase portrait shows arrows bending around both minima, and some paths loop several times before entering a well. Can one of the loops close? Can a coarse sketch reveal an equilibrium that the arrows never get close to? Neither question is settled by inspecting speeds. The Poincaré index extracts an integer from the direction on a boundary. A divergence test constrains the total flux across it. An energy function can then say where a trajectory ends. The damped Duffing oscillator will give us all three diagnostics on the same phase plane.

## When the linear picture loses its authority [L12 00:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CQD2a8ycHis&t=12s)

Let $z=(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2$ be a state and let $\dot z=F(z)$ with a continuously differentiable vector field $F$. An equilibrium $z_*$ satisfies $F(z_*)=0$. The Jacobian $DF(z_*)$ is the matrix of first partial derivatives. A zero real part in its spectrum is a warning that the linear classification may fail. For example, take

$$
\dot x=-y+x(x^2+y^2),\qquad \dot y=x+y(x^2+y^2).
$$

Its linear part predicts closed, unit-frequency rotation on every nonzero circle. For $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}>0$, differentiating $r^2=x^2+y^2$ instead gives $r\dot r=x\dot x+y\dot y=r^4$. The angular velocity is $\dot\theta=(x\dot y-y\dot x)/r^2=1$. As a result,

$$
\frac{1}{r(t)^2}=\frac{1}{r(0)^2}-2t.
$$

Every sufficiently small nonzero initial state eventually leaves any prescribed small disk containing it. Reversing the signs of both cubic terms makes $r(t)\to0$ and gives the same Jacobian. At the origin, the dimension of the slow directions is the same in both models. Their nonlinear radial drift has opposite sign.

The zero Jacobian of $\dot x=x^2-y^2,\ \dot y=2xy$ discards even the angular structure. Write $\zeta=x+iy$, with $i^2=-1$. Then $\dot\zeta=\zeta^2$. On a circle traversed once counterclockwise, $\zeta=\rho e^{i\theta}$, and the vector field direction rotates through $4\pi$ as $\theta$ goes from $0$ to $2\pi$. The origin has index $2$. That observation distinguishes it from a simple saddle, even though both portraits contain incoming and outgoing directions.

## Count zeros by following directions [L12 09:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CQD2a8ycHis&t=599s)

Take a positively oriented piecewise $C^1$ simple closed curve $C$ along which $F=(f,g)$ never vanishes. Assume $F$ is $C^1$ on an open set containing $C$ and its bounded interior. Positive orientation means counterclockwise around that interior. Following $F/\|F\|$ along $C$ makes a closed curve on the unit circle. The index $\operatorname{ind}_C(F)$ is the total change in a continuous choice of argument, divided by $2\pi$. Differentiating the angle in a local chart gives $d\arg(f+ig)=(f\,dg-g\,df)/(f^2+g^2)$, hence

$$
\operatorname{ind}_C(F)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_C
\frac{f\,dg-g\,df}{f^2+g^2}.
$$

The winding number is integer valued because the first and last unit vectors agree. It cannot change under a continuous deformation of $C$ or $F$ during which $F$ never vanishes along the moving boundary. For finitely many isolated interior zeros, remove disjoint small disks around the zeros. Smoothly approximate $F$ uniformly on the remaining compact region. The minimum length of $F$ there is positive, so a sufficiently close approximation can be joined to $F$ by a straight-line deformation through nonvanishing fields. Their winding numbers agree on every boundary component. The smooth field's angle differential is locally exact, so Stokes' theorem equates its outer integral with the sum of its small counterclockwise integrals. The same index equality follows for $F$. An index of zero leaves room for cancellation, and also occurs when there are no zeros in the interior.

For a nondegenerate zero, the local index equals the sign of the determinant of its Jacobian. To see why, write $F(z_*+u)=Au+o(\|u\|)$ with $A=DF(z_*)$. The minimum value of $\|Au\|$ on the unit circle is positive. On a sufficiently small circle the remainder is smaller than the linear term, so interpolating between the exact and linear fields preserves nonvanishing. An invertible linear map takes the circle to an ellipse and preserves or reverses its orientation according to $\det A$. This gives $+1$ for ordinary nodes, spirals and linear centers, and $-1$ for a saddle. A sink and source can both have index $+1$. Index counts net direction winding and does not record attraction.

If $C$ is itself a nonconstant periodic orbit, uniqueness makes it a simple closed trajectory over one minimal period. Its field never vanishes and is tangent along the curve. The tangent to a positively oriented regular Jordan curve turns through $2\pi$. Even when the actual orbit travels clockwise, along the counterclockwise parametrization $F$ differs from the positive tangent by a negative scalar everywhere. The extra angle $\pi$ does not affect the total turn. Thus a planar periodic orbit requires total interior index $+1$. A node and saddle can coalesce in a saddle-node with net index zero because a boundary enclosing both keeps the same degree through the collision.

## The Duffing phase plane gives two different obstruction tests [L12 38:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CQD2a8ycHis&t=2286s)

Use the dimensionless unforced Duffing example $\dot x=y,\ \dot y=x-x^3-\gamma y$ with drag $\gamma\ge0$. The equilibria have $y=0$ and $x\in\{-1,0,1\}$. At such a point the Jacobian determinant is $3x^2-1$. Both outer points have index $+1$. The origin has index $-1$, so a boundary enclosing all three has index $+1$. Index theory alone permits a periodic orbit enclosing all three and also permits one surrounding a single outer equilibrium. The energy function

$$
H(x,y)=\frac{y^2}{2}+U(x),\qquad U(x)=\frac{x^4}{4}-\frac{x^2}{2}
$$

settles the distinction between zero and positive drag. With $\gamma=0$, the minima of $U$ at $x=\pm1$ lie below the barrier $U(0)=0$. Energies between $-1/4$ and $0$ yield two disconnected families of periodic ovals. For $H=0$ the separatrix consists of two homoclinic loops, which are trajectories that approach the same saddle in both time directions. At $x\to0$ on the separatrix, $|y|\sim|x|$, so the elapsed time contains a divergent logarithmic integral of $dx/|x|$. The loops are not periodic trajectories through the saddle.

With $\gamma>0$, the chain rule gives $\dot H=y\dot y+U'(x)\dot x=-\gamma y^2$. Integration over a putative period gives $\int y^2\,dt=0$, so $y=0$ along the entire path and the path is an equilibrium. There is a second proof at the level of the vector field. Its divergence $\partial_x f+\partial_y g$ is $-\gamma$. By Green's theorem the flux through a closed curve equals the area integral of the divergence over its interior. Flux is zero on a periodic orbit since $F$ is tangent. A strictly negative divergence throughout the interior forces strictly negative flux. This form of the Bendixson criterion also applies to a $C^1$ planar system on a simply connected domain with divergence of one sign and nonzero somewhere in every open disk. A hole inside a candidate curve invalidates the area-integral argument over the full interior.

## Action measures area and its energy derivative measures time [L13 00:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AoXLncutE5g&t=13s)

For a Hamiltonian $H(q,p)=p^2/(2m)+U(q)$ with $m>0$, the canonical coordinate $q$ and conjugate momentum $p$ satisfy $\dot q=p/m$ and $\dot p=-U'(q)$. On a regular oscillatory energy level $E$ with two simple turning points $q_-(E)<q_+(E)$ and $U(q)<E$ throughout the interval between them, define the action by $I(E)=(2\pi)^{-1}\oint p\,dq$ in time orientation. The integral subtracts the lower momentum branch from the upper one, giving enclosed area divided by $2\pi$:

$$
I(E)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{q_-}^{q_+}\sqrt{2m(E-U(q))}\,dq .
$$

At regular turning points the endpoint contributions vanish when differentiating. Inside the interval, differentiating the square root gives $m/\sqrt{2m(E-U)}$, the time per distance on the upper branch. The singularity at a simple turning point is integrable. Therefore $dI/dE=T(E)/(2\pi)$, where $T$ is the full period. At a separatrix the action can have a finite limiting value even when its derivative diverges. For the unit-mass Duffing right loop at $E=0$, the upper momentum is $p=q\sqrt{1-q^2/2}$ on $[0,\sqrt2]$. Integration gives $I(0^-)=2/(3\pi)$. The one-sided slope grows without bound as $E\uparrow0$ because the trajectory spends ever more time close to the saddle. For a harmonic potential $U(q)=m\omega^2q^2/2$, the energy ellipse has area $2\pi E/\omega$, giving $I=E/\omega$ and $T=2\pi/\omega$.

## Recognize conserved and decreasing functions [L13 15:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AoXLncutE5g&t=954s)

On a simply connected region of the plane, a $C^1$ field $F=(f,g)$ is canonical Hamiltonian with $F=(\partial_y H,-\partial_x H)$ exactly when $f_x+g_y=0$. Indeed, $dH=-g\,dx+f\,dy$ is closed precisely under that compatibility condition, and simple connectivity makes it exact. A negative gradient field $F=-\nabla V$ instead requires $f_y=g_x$. For the lecture field

$$
(f,g)=(-y+xy,\ x+x^2/2-y^2/2)
$$

one has $f_x=y$ and $g_y=-y$. Integrating $H_y=f$ and matching $-H_x=g$ gives $H=(x-1)y^2/2-x^2/2-x^3/6$, up to a constant. The gradient condition fails because $f_y=x-1$ and $g_x=x+1$. If both structures do occur in a region, $\nabla H$ and $\nabla V$ are perpendicular along regular trajectories, and $\Delta V=0$. A harmonic function has its value at the center of a disk equal to its circumference average, so a nonconstant harmonic function cannot have an interior local minimum. The mean-value property connects the local compatibility calculation to the absence of a potential well in a charge-free electrostatic region.

A nonlinear circuit exhibits a conserved energy by a different route. Let $Q$ denote capacitor charge, $C>0$ the capacitance, $i=\dot Q$ the current and $\Phi(i)$ the flux linkage of a memoryless inductor. Kirchhoff's voltage law gives $\Phi'(i)\dot i+Q/C=0$. Multiplication by $i$ yields the conserved energy

$$
\mathcal E=\frac{Q^2}{2C}+\int_0^i u\Phi'(u)\,du.
$$

The inductive term is the area $\int u\,d\Phi$, and it equals $Li^2/2$ when $\Phi(i)=Li$. If $\Phi'(i)>0$, use the canonical momentum $p=\Phi(i)$ with Hamiltonian $Q^2/(2C)+\int_{\Phi(0)}^p \Phi^{-1}(v)\,dv$. Differentiation with respect to $p$ returns $i$. Treating current as the momentum would generally lose the correct relation between energy and velocity.

## Use a potential well to decide when a center exists [L13 45:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AoXLncutE5g&t=2710s)

For a dimensionless restoring-force oscillator $\dot x=y,\ \dot y=-s(x)$, suppose $s$ is $C^1$, $s(0)=0$ and $s'(x)>0$ near zero. Then $W(x)=\int_0^x s(u)\,du$ has a strict minimum at zero and is monotone on either side. Each sufficiently low positive energy $E=y^2/2+W(x)$ gives two turning points $x_-<0<x_+$ with nonzero $s$ there. The energy contour is a closed regular curve and $F$ is tangent and nonvanishing on it. The time to traverse it is $\sqrt{2}\int_{x_-}^{x_+}(E-W(x))^{-1/2}\,dx$. Both endpoints have integrable square-root singularities. This shows there is a family of periodic orbits with the origin as a center, and specifies what controls their periods. A planar linear system $\dot z=Az$ has a nondegenerate center exactly when $\operatorname{tr} A=0$ and $\det A>0$, because its characteristic polynomial is then $\lambda^2+\det A$ with distinct imaginary roots. For a nonlinear system with the same Jacobian, the cubic radial example shows why a conserved well supplies information absent from the linear conditions alone.

## Stress-test assumptions, then use Lyapunov's direct method [L14 00:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8eKFUwPu4MM&t=12s) · [L14 27:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8eKFUwPu4MM&t=1633s)

The [official first quiz](https://archive.nptel.ac.in/content/storage2/courses/115106059/downloads/quiz1.pdf) brings together common failure modes: conclusions about attraction from energy conservation, the limits of local linearization, compatibility of canonical coordinates, and transitivity of vanishing Poisson brackets. For example, with $\{A,B\}=A_qB_p-A_pB_q$, take $A=q$, $B=1$, $C=p$. Both brackets involving $B$ vanish while $\{A,C\}=1$. The Jacobi identity instead shows $\{\{A,C\},B\}=0$ when the other two brackets vanish identically. Their failure to imply $\{A,C\}=0$ is a failure of transitivity, with no contradiction in the identity.

The quiz also raises a subtle limit on isolated-cycle arguments. In polar coordinates consider $\dot r=e^{-1/r^2}\sin(1/r^2)$ and $\dot\theta=1$ for $r>0$. At $r=0$ extend the Cartesian vector field continuously by zero. The radial term is flat because the exponential dominates every inverse power of $r$, so the Cartesian extension is smooth. Circular orbits occur at $r_n=(n\pi)^{-1/2}$ for integers $n\ge1$. At $r_n$, the radial derivative is $-2r_n^{-3}e^{-1/r_n^2}(-1)^n$, so even $n$ gives radial attraction and odd $n$ repulsion. Each cycle is isolated in its own neighborhood even though an infinite sequence accumulates at the equilibrium. This smooth teaching variant retains the quiz's alternating stability mechanism.

A $C^1$ function $V$ is positive definite relative to the equilibrium if $V(z_*)=0$ and $V(z)>0$ for other states in a neighborhood. If $\dot V=\nabla V\cdot F\le0$ there, choose a small sphere around $z_*$ inside the neighborhood. The minimum of $V$ on the sphere is positive, and a trajectory starting at a smaller value cannot reach the sphere. This proves Lyapunov stability. If $\dot V$ is strictly negative away from $z_*$, the orbit converges: on any compact band of positive $V$ values in the trapped region, $\dot V$ is uniformly bounded above by a negative constant. A positive limiting value would force the trajectory to lose that much energy per unit time indefinitely.

For the damped Duffing oscillator in the right well, take $V=H+1/4$. Choose a sublevel strictly below the barrier and its component containing $(1,0)$. It is compact, positively invariant, and $V$ is positive definite relative to its minimum. Although $\dot V=0$ on the whole $x$-axis, a trajectory on this axis can stay there only if $x-x^3=0$. Inside the chosen component the only such point is $(1,0)$. LaSalle's invariance theorem applies: the forward limit set of a trapped orbit lies in the largest invariant subset of $\{\dot V=0\}$. Continuity makes $V$ constant on that limit set, since the monotone values along the orbit have a limit. Invariance then makes its derivative zero there. The only possible limit is $(1,0)$. This gives convergence from sublevel trapping without requiring a strictly decreasing value at every instant or claiming attraction across the barrier. [LaSalle's original theorem](https://www.et.byu.edu/~beard/papers/library/LaSalle60a.pdf)

6. **Distinguish stable from attracting.** The oscillator $\dot x=y,\ \dot y=-x^3$ has a zero-real-part linearization. Use an energy function to decide both stability and asymptotic attraction at the origin.

4. Take $H=xy$ and $V=(-x^2+y^2)/2$. Then $(H_y,-H_x)=(x,-y)=-\nabla V$. Along the flow, $\dot H=xy-xy=0$ while $\dot V=-x^2-y^2$. Their gradients are perpendicular wherever both are nonzero. In particular $V$ is not positive definite, so its decrease provides no stability claim for the saddle.
5. Let $V=v^2/2+1-\cos\theta$. Its derivative is $-\gamma v^2$. In the sublevel component with $V\le c<2$ around the bottom, $v$ and $\theta$ are bounded. The only complete invariant trajectory in the intersection of this component and $v=0$ is the bottom equilibrium, since $v$ can stay zero only where $\sin\theta=0$. 6. $V=y^2/2+x^4/4$ is positive definite and $\dot V=0$. This gives stability by the sublevel argument. Every nearby nonzero initial condition has positive energy, which stays constant. Convergence to the origin would force the energy to approach zero by continuity. Thus the origin is stable with no nearby nonconstant orbit converging to it.
