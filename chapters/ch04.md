# Action–angle coordinates, separatrices, and Hamiltonian symmetry

The timing of an inertial sensor can be calibrated by watching a small oscillation. If the same sensor makes a large excursion, the calibration can fail even in the ideal absence of friction. An energy level tells us where the sensor moves. To obtain its period and to understand how motion changes when two oscillators interact, we need the phase-space area and the return geometry of the orbit.

## Recover the orbit and its time from one energy level ([L7 10:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=haN6NswqI58&t=618s))

Let $q\in\mathbb R$ be a generalized position and $p\in\mathbb R$ its conjugate momentum. Take $H(q,p)=p^2/(2m)+V(q)$ with $m>0$ and smooth potential $V$. Energy $E=H$ is constant. On the level where motion stays in a well, assume two simple roots $q_-<q_+$ of $V(q)=E$ bound a region where $V(q)<E$. The two branches are $p_\pm(q)=\pm\sqrt{2m[E-V(q)]}$. Time increases in the direction of $p$ because $\dot q=p/m$. On the upper branch

$$
dt=\frac{m\,dq}{\sqrt{2m[E-V(q)]}},\qquad
T(E)=2\int_{q_-}^{q_+}\frac{m\,dq}{\sqrt{2m[E-V(q)]}} .
$$

The factor $2$ accounts for the return on the lower branch. Each endpoint is integrable since a simple turning point gives a square-root denominator. At that point $p=0$ while $\dot p=-V'(q)$ is nonzero, so the trajectory crosses the $p=0$ line and reverses direction in $q$. A stationary point of $V$ at the same energy can instead give an equilibrium with infinite approach time. The energy integral then needs to be examined again.

For $V(q)=m\omega^2q^2/2$ with $\omega>0$, the substitution $q=q_+\sin u$ cancels the square root, giving $T=2\pi/\omega$ at every positive energy. This property is called **isochrony**. For a nondegenerate minimum $q_0$, the first nonconstant term of a Taylor expansion is $V''(q_0)(q-q_0)^2/2$. The quadratic predicts the small-amplitude period $2\pi\sqrt{m/V''(q_0)}$. Higher derivatives enter at finite amplitude. A flat minimum with $V''(q_0)=0$ invalidates the linear frequency estimate. In a double well, different connected components at the same energy can describe different orbits. Energy and a component label together specify the orbit geometry. [Lecture 7, energy levels and turning points](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=haN6NswqI58&t=1036s)

## A pendulum slows down near its separatrix ([L7 39:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=haN6NswqI58&t=2378s))

Use the angle $q$ measured from the hanging position and identify angles modulo $2\pi$. The phase space is then a cylinder in $(q,p)$. Let $m$ be the bob mass, $\ell$ the length, $g$ the gravitational acceleration, and $p=m\ell^2\dot q$. Define

$$
H=\frac{p^2}{2m\ell^2}+mg\ell(1-\cos q).
$$

The hanging state $(0,0)$ is a minimum and the upright state $(\pi,0)$ a saddle. At energy $E<2mg\ell$ and $E>0$, an orbit in the well oscillates between turning angles $\pm q_{\max}$, a motion called **libration**. For $E>2mg\ell$ its momentum has fixed sign and the angle rotates around the cylinder. At the intermediate energy the level contains the saddle and forms a **separatrix**, the invariant boundary between libration and rotation. The branches approach the same upright equilibrium asymptotically when the angle is taken modulo $2\pi$.

Write $k=\sin(q_{\max}/2)$. The identity $1-\cos q=2\sin^2(q/2)$ and the substitution $\sin(q/2)=k\sin u$ reduce the period to

$$
T(k)=4\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}}\int_0^{\pi/2}
\frac{du}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2u}}.
$$

At $k=0$ the integral is $\pi/2$ and the linearized period is recovered as a limit. At $k\to1$ the denominator near $u=\pi/2$ scales like $\sqrt{(1-k^2)+(\pi/2-u)^2}$. Its reciprocal integrates to a logarithm, so the time diverges. A trajectory can cover much the same angular distance and still spend arbitrarily long near the upright saddle. The harmonic calibration misses this effect. [Lecture 8, pendulum period](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MZ9tnuRaVNY&t=16s)

## Action is area divided by one full turn ([L8 15:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MZ9tnuRaVNY&t=918s))

For a regular oscillatory orbit with two simple turning points, define its **action**

$$
I(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint p\,dq
=\frac1\pi\int_{q_-}^{q_+}\sqrt{2m[E-V(q)]}\,dq.
$$

The contour runs in the forward direction. For this orbit the integral gives the enclosed area divided by $2\pi$, with units of momentum times position. A rotating pendulum can also have a closed orbit on the cylinder, but it winds around the cylinder without enclosing a disk. Its contour integral uses one full angular turn in a fixed rotation direction, without the turning-point formula. In a smooth family of oscillatory orbits with simple turning points, differentiating with respect to $E$ produces no endpoint term because the integrand vanishes there. The remaining square-root singularities are integrable. The same quadrature appears in the period, giving

$$
\frac{dI}{dE}=\frac{T(E)}{2\pi},\qquad
\Omega(I)=\frac{dE}{dI}=\frac{2\pi}{T(E)} .
$$

The conjugate **angle** $\theta$ is taken modulo $2\pi$. In canonical coordinates $(\theta,I)$ with $H=E(I)$, Hamilton's equations give $\dot I=0$ and $\dot\theta=\Omega(I)$. A pointwise force law becomes uniform translation on a circle, whose speed depends on the action. In the harmonic case $I=E/\omega$. One explicit transformation for $I>0$ is $q=\sqrt{2I/(m\omega)}\sin\theta$, $p=\sqrt{2Im\omega}\cos\theta$. Differentiation yields $dq\wedge dp=d\theta\wedge dI$, where the wedge denotes the oriented area form. The singularities at the zero-amplitude equilibrium and on the pendulum separatrix explain why a single regular angle chart cannot cover an entire phase portrait. [Dunajski, action–angle variables, §1.2](https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/md327/ISlecture_notes_2026.pdf)

For two uncoupled oscillators with actions $I_1,I_2$ fixed, the two angles range over a torus $(\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z)^2$. If their positive frequencies are $\omega_1,\omega_2$, an exact return requires $\omega_j T$ to be an integer multiple of $2\pi$ for each $j$. For $\omega_2/\omega_1=3/2$, the least positive return time is $4\pi/\omega_1$. To understand the irrational case, sample the second angle each time the first angle completes a turn. The second angle advances by $2\pi\omega_2/\omega_1$ modulo $2\pi$. Irrational rotations have dense orbits: by splitting the circle into arbitrarily short arcs and comparing enough iterates, two must lie in one arc. Their difference gives a nonzero step of arbitrarily small magnitude, itself a multiple of the original rotation. Repeating this small step runs within its magnitude of every point of the circle. Time averaging also distinguishes the two cases. For a Fourier mode $e^{i(k_1\theta_1+k_2\theta_2)}$ with integer $(k_1,k_2)\ne(0,0)$, its average tends to zero when $k_1\omega_1+k_2\omega_2\ne0$, by direct integration of the exponential. Trigonometric-polynomial approximation extends this to continuous observables. Thus irrational flow is equidistributed on its own torus, with actions fixed. [Lecture 8, commensurability](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MZ9tnuRaVNY&t=691s). [Lecture 8, torus motion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MZ9tnuRaVNY&t=1213s)

When a small Hamiltonian coupling is introduced, preservation of a torus needs further hypotheses. In a classical Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser setting the frequency map of the integrable system must satisfy a nondegeneracy condition, and the frequencies of persisting tori obey quantitative nonresonance bounds. The uncoupled harmonic model has constant frequencies, so its action Hessian vanishes and fails the usual Kolmogorov condition. Irrational ratios alone are insufficient to apply the theorem. [Lecture 8, sensitivity to perturbations](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MZ9tnuRaVNY&t=1897s). [Arnol'd, proof of Kolmogorov's theorem](https://www.mathnet.ru/php/archive.phtml?jrnid=rm&option_lang=eng&paperid=6404&wshow=paper)

## Power-law wells let the size of an orbit set its clock ([L8 40:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MZ9tnuRaVNY&t=2414s))

Let $V(q)=a|q|^s$ with $a>0$ and an even integer $s\ge2$, so the well is smooth at zero. For energy $E>0$, its positive turning point is $(E/a)^{1/s}$. Rescale position as $q=(E/a)^{1/s}u$ in the action integral. By symmetry,

$$
I(E)=\frac{2\sqrt{2m}}{\pi a^{1/s}}
   E^{1/2+1/s}\int_0^1\sqrt{1-u^s}\,du .
$$

The dimensionless integral depends only on $s$. Consequently $T(E)\propto E^{1/s-1/2}$. There is no energy dependence for $s=2$. A quartic well has $T\propto E^{-1/4}$, so the period increases without bound as the amplitude tends to zero. There is no harmonic term to set a finite small-signal frequency. In the semiclassical aside, an approximate rule that spaces neighboring actions uniformly gives $E_j\propto (j+\alpha)^{2s/(s+2)}$ for a level index $j$ and offset $\alpha$. The classical scaling carries the exponent. It does not provide exact quantum energies or determine the phase offset. [Lecture 9, semiclassical scaling](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqGKB3Rd5g0&t=38s)

## A continuous symmetry gives an additional invariant ([L9 11:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqGKB3Rd5g0&t=695s))

In canonical variables, let a smooth function $G$ generate a one-parameter flow by $\partial_s z=J\nabla G$ with group parameter $s$. This is itself a Hamiltonian flow in $s$. If $H$ is unchanged along this transformation, then $\partial_s H=\{H,G\}=0$. Antisymmetry gives $dG/dt=\{G,H\}=0$ along the physical Hamiltonian flow for a time-independent $G$. This gives a way to find first integrals from symmetries of the Hamiltonian. The symplectic condition may be written $M^TJM=J$ or $MJM^T=J$ for the invertible derivative matrix $M$ of a transform. To see equivalence, invert either equality and use $J^{-1}=-J$. The unit determinant follows because a symplectic map preserves the top exterior power of the canonical two-form, which gives the oriented volume. [Lecture 9, symplectic matrices](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqGKB3Rd5g0&t=1163s)

For the dimensionless planar oscillator $H=(q_1^2+q_2^2+p_1^2+p_2^2)/2$, simultaneous rotations of $q$ and $p$ preserve $H$. Their generator is $L=q_1p_2-q_2p_1$. Its Hamiltonian field has $\partial_s q=(-q_2,q_1)$ and $\partial_s p=(-p_2,p_1)$, which rotates each vector. The individual energies $E_j=(q_j^2+p_j^2)/2$ commute and are constant. Define also

$$
A=(E_1-E_2)/2,\qquad B=(q_1q_2+p_1p_2)/2,\qquad C=L/2 .
$$

The two bilinear invariants describe the relative phase of the oscillators. Taking the bracket of $A$ and $B$ gives $(q_1p_2-q_2p_1)/2=C$. Taking the two cyclic brackets gives $\{B,C\}=A$ and $\{C,A\}=B$. Each function commutes with $H$, but the three need not commute with each other. Their values are also related by $A^2+B^2+C^2=H^2/4$ because $(q_1q_2+p_1p_2)^2+(q_1p_2-q_2p_1)^2=4E_1E_2$. Generically $E_1,E_2$ and one relative-phase invariant can be independent. A system with more independent first integrals than its degrees of freedom is **superintegrable**. Here the equal oscillator frequencies keep the phase difference constant and all nonzero trajectories close after one common period. [Lecture 9, isotropic oscillator](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dqGKB3Rd5g0&t=2532s)

## Laboratory

The [action and period lab](../code/ch04.py) uses quadrature to compare a finite-difference estimate of $2\pi\,dI/dE$ with a direct period calculation and tests the exact quartic scaling. It also checks the harmonic limit and the finite-amplitude increase of the pendulum period, and tests the relations between the isotropic oscillator invariants. Coefficients are fixed dimensionless values. Near the pendulum separatrix the integrand becomes increasingly sharp, so the code only tests parameters bounded away from $k=1$.

## Exercises

1. For $H=p^2/(2m)+m\omega^2q^2/2$, calculate the action by the ellipse area. Derive the frequency using its inverse relation $H=H(I)$.
2. In the quartic well $V(q)=a q^4$, a particle's positive turning point doubles. How do the energy, action, and period scale?
3. For two oscillators with frequencies $4$ and $6$ in inverse time units, find the least positive common period. Which property of the frequency vector prevents an exact return for $(4,4\sqrt 2)$?
4. Show from the action integral that for the pendulum's libration $I(E)$ stays finite as $E\to2mg\ell$ from below, even though $dI/dE$ diverges.
5. Show directly from the bracket that $L=q_1p_2-q_2p_1$ remains constant for any planar central Hamiltonian $H=\|p\|^2/(2m)+V(r)$ with $r=\|q\|>0$.
6. For the two-dimensional unit oscillator, take $q_1=\cos t$, $p_1=-\sin t$, $q_2=2\cos(t+\delta)$, $p_2=-2\sin(t+\delta)$. Compute $A,B,C$ and use them to recover $\delta$ modulo $2\pi$.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Worked solutions</summary>

1. The semiaxes are $\sqrt{2E/(m\omega^2)}$ in $q$ and $\sqrt{2mE}$ in $p$. Multiplying by $\pi$ gives area $2\pi E/\omega$, so $I=E/\omega$ and $dE/dI=\omega$.
2. Doubling the turning point multiplies $E$ by $2^4=16$. The action scales like $E^{3/4}$, so its factor is $16^{3/4}=8$. The period scales like $E^{-1/4}$, hence it is halved. The larger restoring force at the larger excursion is reflected in the increased angular frequency.
3. We need $4T=2\pi k_1$ and $6T=2\pi k_2$ with positive integers $k_1,k_2$. The smallest solution uses $(k_1,k_2)=(2,3)$, giving $T=\pi$. The irrational frequency ratio $\sqrt 2$ cannot equal a ratio of the integers required for an exact return.
4. The interval of libration stays within $[-\pi,\pi]$. The integrand $p_+(q)=\sqrt{2m\ell^2[E-mg\ell(1-\cos q)]}$ is at most $2m\ell\sqrt{g\ell}$ there. Extend it by zero outside the turning points and use dominated convergence. The integrand tends to $2m\ell\sqrt{g\ell}\cos(q/2)$ on the interval, with finite integral. Differentiating with respect to energy instead gives an inverse square root, which behaves like $1/(\pi-|q|)$ at the limiting endpoints and causes the logarithmic divergence.
5. The derivatives of $L$ are $\partial_q L=(p_2,-p_1)$ and $\partial_p L=(-q_2,q_1)$. The first is perpendicular to $\partial_p H=p/m$. The second is perpendicular to $\partial_q H=V'(r)q/r$. Both terms in the Poisson bracket vanish.
6. The energies are $E_1=1/2$, $E_2=2$, giving $A=-3/4$. The cosine addition formula gives $B=\cos\delta$ and the sine difference formula gives $C=-\sin\delta$. Hence $\delta$ modulo $2\pi$ is the polar angle of $(B,-C)$. The physical phase along the orbit still depends on the time origin.

</details>
