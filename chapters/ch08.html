Chapter 8 · Lectures 17–18

One-dimensional maps and period doubling

L17 L18

Suppose an instrument reports a normalized state once per cycle. Its next reading is a deterministic function of the current reading. A programmer who sees the readings alternate between two values might expect two stable operating points. That diagnosis misses a possibility: the instrument may have one attracting orbit with two distinct phases. This chapter uses three interval maps to distinguish convergence to a fixed point, periodic alternation and sensitivity to small errors.

Fixed points of an iterate can belong to a cycle L17 00:16

Let \(f:I\to I\) be a map on an interval \(I\), and let \(x_n=f^n(x_0)\) be the state after \(n\) steps, where \(f^n\) means composition \(n\) times and \(f^0\) is the identity. A fixed point satisfies \(f(x)=x\). A point of least period \(p\ge1\) satisfies \(f^p(x)=x\) and \(f^k(x)\ne x\) for \(1\le k<p\). The qualifier “least” matters. A point of period \(2\) is fixed by every even iterate, and the two roots generated by one two-cycle represent different start phases of one orbit.

The multiplier \(M_p\) of a differentiable period-\(p\) orbit is the derivative of the \(p\)-step map at one orbit point. The chain rule gives

\[M_p=(f^p)'(x_0)=\prod_{j=0}^{p-1}f'(x_j).\]

The product is unchanged by cycling the starting point. If \(|M_p|<1\), a sufficiently small perturbation contracts each time it returns to the same phase, and continuity of the intermediate maps gives local attraction to the cycle. If \(|M_p|>1\), repeated returns amplify small perturbations. A single step can expand even when the full cycle attracts. When \(|M_p|=1\), further terms in the return map are needed.

The cobweb construction alternates a vertical step from \((x_n,x_n)\) to \((x_n,f(x_n))\) with a horizontal step to \((f(x_n),f(x_n))\). The diagonal returns the output to the input axis. For the model \(f(x)=1-x/2\) on \([0,1]\), \(x_*=2/3\), and the errors satisfy \(x_{n+1}-x_*=-(x_n-x_*)/2\). Starting at zero gives \(0,1,1/2,3/4,5/8,\ldots\): repeated alternation about a single fixed point. A genuine period-two diagnosis needs two limiting values a positive distance apart.

The dimension of the original flow matters when using a map to study whether trajectories can move between regions. For a regular invariant \(n\)-torus inside a regular \((2n-1)\)-dimensional energy surface, the codimension inside that surface is \(n-1\). With two degrees of freedom, a torus has codimension one, so a small neighborhood of a point on it has two local sides. A trajectory cannot pass through the torus because uniqueness would identify it with the trajectory already on the torus. With three or more degrees of freedom the codimension is at least two. A small neighborhood permits paths around the torus within the energy surface. This dimension count describes local geometry. Whether trajectories can follow such paths, and whether a torus separates the whole energy surface, need information about the flow and the topology. L17 04:17

The doubling map exposes lost information L17 17:19

On \([0,1)\) take \(D(x)=2x-\lfloor 2x\rfloor\). Each target \(y\) has two preimages, \(y/2\) and \((y+1)/2\). Induction gives \(D^p(x)=2^p x\bmod 1\). A fixed point of \(D^p\) has \(x=k/(2^p-1)\) with \(k=0,\ldots,2^p-2\). The top endpoint \(1\) is outside our chosen domain. For \(p=2\) the three fixed points are \(0,1/3,2/3\). The nonzero pair is one two-cycle. Its multiplier is \(2^2=4\), so it repels nearby points despite repeating forever for exact initial data.

Writing \(x=0.b_1b_2\ldots\) in binary makes the operation literal: \(D\) drops \(b_1\) and shifts the remaining digits left. Use the expansion with trailing zeros for dyadic rationals, numbers with denominator a power of two. The dropped bits explain why an exact present cannot reconstruct the past even though it determines the future. From a given \(D^n(x)\), every word of \(n\) bits reconstructs a different preimage.

For a rational starting point, repeated doubling takes place among finitely many numerator residues modulo its denominator. The orbit is eventually periodic, and it enters zero precisely when the reduced denominator is a power of two. Binary words of length \(p\) repeated periodically give the points fixed by \(D^p\), excluding the all-ones representation of the endpoint \(1\). The periodic points are dense because repeating any finite prefix followed by a zero produces a point in the corresponding half-open dyadic interval. This set is countable and has zero Lebesgue measure.

For uniform choice of \(x_0\), a specified block of \(k\) leading bits has probability \(2^{-k}\). Blocks inspected at nonoverlapping positions are independent equally likely words. For a fixed word, the probability of missing it in \(N\) independent trials is \((1-2^{-k})^N\), tending to zero. A countable union over all finite words shows that almost every orbit visits every dyadic interval. This is an almost-everywhere conclusion and cannot follow from irrationality alone. An infinite binary expansion with ones only at positions \(2^j\), for integers \(j\ge1\), is aperiodic with growing gaps and no adjacent ones. It gives an irrational initial point whose orbit never enters the dyadic interval \([3/4,1)\). L17 26:11

Measure the growth of a tangent error L17 46:37

For a differentiable orbit where the relevant derivatives are nonzero, let \(\lambda(x_0)\) be the limiting logarithmic expansion per iterate,

\[\lambda(x_0)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log|(f^N)'(x_0)| =\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\log|f'(x_j)| ,\]

if the limit exists. If the orbit hits a point of zero derivative, the finite product is zero from there onward and its logarithm is \(-\infty\) in the extended sense. The derivative limit takes the initial error to zero for fixed \(N\) before sending \(N\) to infinity. Distances between two finite-error trajectories in a bounded interval cannot grow exponentially without end. In \(D\), branchwise displacements double exactly until a branch boundary intervenes. The exponent is \(\log 2\) on orbits avoiding the discontinuity in interval coordinates, including the repelling two-cycle. A positive exponent for that cycle describes its tangent instability. It does not turn its exact periodic itinerary into an irregular one.

For a regular period-\(p\) point, the sequence of log slopes repeats. Averaging over repetitions gives \(\lambda=p^{-1}\log|M_p|\). For a nonperiodic trajectory the corresponding empirical average can fluctuate over finite time. A transient average, even when positive, does not establish a limiting positive exponent. The laboratory uses exact fractions for the binary map because finite binary floating-point inputs eventually acquire an all-zero tail.

Folding changes the map while preserving its expansion mechanism L18 00:30

The symmetric tent family on \([0,1]\) is \(T_r(x)=2r\min\{x,1-x\}\) with \(0\le r\le1\). Its two branches join at \(x=1/2\) with opposite slopes. The tent folds the interval, whereas the doubling map cuts and stacks the branches of the graph. At \(r=1\), either branch lands onto the full interval and has absolute slope \(2\). Both maps then exhibit tangent expansion \(\log 2\) away from points that encounter a branch singularity.

For \(r<1/2\), \(T_r(x)\le2rx\) shows global attraction to zero. At \(r=1/2\), the first half is fixed pointwise and the second half enters it after one step. For \(r>1/2\), zero repels and a fixed point on the descending branch satisfies \(x_*=2r(1-x_*)\), hence \(x_*=2r/(1+2r)>1/2\). Its slope is \(-2r\), so it repels as well. For any orbit that avoids the cusp, the magnitude of the derivative is constant, giving \(\lambda=\log(2r)\) for \(r>0\). A cusp encounter is excluded from that derivative calculation. The change in sign of the exponent at \(r=1/2\) involves a flat band of marginal fixed points. This piecewise-linear family has different critical geometry from the smooth logistic map. L18 08:32

A fixed point flips into a two-cycle in the logistic family L18 23:54

The smooth map \(f_\mu(x)=\mu x(1-x)\) takes \([0,1]\) into itself for \(0\le\mu\le4\). The maximum at \(x=1/2\) is \(\mu/4\), giving the upper constraint. One interpretation is a population normalized by a carrying scale, with crowded states reducing the next generation. The model encodes this assumption in the quadratic term. Its iterates are well defined even when its biological interpretation is set aside. May's paper uses this and related difference equations to show how simple recurrence laws can lead from equilibrium to cycles and irregular dynamics. May, Simple Mathematical Models with Very Complicated Dynamics

Solving \(x=\mu x(1-x)\) gives \(x=0\) and, for \(\mu\ge1\), \(x_*=1-1/\mu\). The derivative \(f_\mu'(x)=\mu(1-2x)\) equals \(\mu\) at zero and \(2-\mu\) at \(x_*\). For \(0\le\mu<1\), zero attracts. For \(1<\mu<3\), \(x_*\) attracts locally and zero repels. At \(\mu=1\), the two branches meet and exchange stability in the extended model on the real line. At \(\mu=3\), \(x_*\) first reaches multiplier \(-1\). A displacement flips sign with each step, so its two-step multiplier is then \(+1\).

Rather than approximating a new cycle from a plot, factor the fixed-point equation for the second iterate. For \(\mu>0\) a direct expansion gives

\[f_\mu^2(x)-x =-\mu x\left(x-\frac{\mu-1}{\mu}\right) \left(\mu^2x^2-\mu(\mu+1)x+\mu+1\right).\]

The quadratic factor gives two distinct real roots for \(\mu>3\):

\[x_\pm=\frac{\mu+1\pm\sqrt{(\mu-3)(\mu+1)}}{2\mu}.\]

They cannot be fixed individually, so the map must exchange them. There is one new period-two attractor when its multiplier is between \(-1\) and \(1\). To compute it, use the quadratic root sum \(x_++x_-=(\mu+1)/\mu\) and product \(x_+x_-=(\mu+1)/\mu^2\). Thus

\[M_2=\mu^2(1-2x_+)(1-2x_-)=-\mu^2+2\mu+4.\]

It is \(+1\) at \(\mu=3\), then drops until it is \(-1\) at \(\mu=1+\sqrt6\). The attracting two-cycle therefore persists for \(3<\mu<1+\sqrt6\). Its branch separation is \(\sqrt{(\mu-3)(\mu+1)}/\mu\), growing as a square root at onset. Unlike the hoop pitchfork, the two branches are exchanged by the one-step evolution. Their attraction is a property of the second iterate. L18 37:21

Distinguish parameter accumulation, windows, and escape L18 41:30

The same return-map mechanism repeats when a cycle loses stability through multiplier \(-1\). The logistic cascade has successive attracting cycles of period \(2,4,8,\ldots\) with parameters converging to a finite accumulation point. Feigenbaum studied a class of recurrences with a single smooth maximum. For quadratic maxima the paper reports an asymptotic parameter ratio, measured using the sequence of superstable cycles containing the maximum, of approximately \(4.6692\). A cycle is superstable in this convention when the multiplier is zero. This is a statement about a class and a limit. A ratio formed from the first two of this map's thresholds is not a measurement of the limiting constant. Feigenbaum, Quantitative Universality for a Class of Nonlinear Transformations

Above the first accumulation point, parameter sweeps contain windows with attracting cycles. In the period-three window, for example, a new stable and unstable three-cycle come together at a tangent bifurcation of \(f_\mu^3\) at the window's lower boundary. The stable three-cycle can then undergo its own doublings. Therefore an observation that an earlier family of \(2^k\)-cycles has lost stability does not classify every later parameter as chaotic. L18 52:19

For \(\mu>4\), a centered interval of starting points maps above one. Solving \(\mu x(1-x)>1\) identifies it as \((a,1-a)\) with \(a=(1-\sqrt{1-4/\mu})/2\). The next iterate is negative. Once \(x<0\), \(|\mu x(1-x)|>\mu|x|\), so each subsequent magnitude grows without bound. Initial states surviving in \([0,1]\) must avoid the hole and every preimage of the hole. Successive branch preimages carve intervals repeatedly, providing a geometric route to Cantor-like surviving sets. This construction shows why an observed escape is compatible with the existence of a smaller invariant surviving set. L18 59:42

Laboratory

The doubling and logistic lab uses exact fractions to enumerate the doubling-map two-cycle. It checks the logistic cycle formula, the multiplier product, and the outcome of transient removal for one starting point. At \(\mu=3.2\), it obtains the two states \(0.513045\) and \(0.799455\) and multiplier \(0.160000\). This verifies the analytic cycle and its local attraction numerically. A long iterate of a different parameter can have a transient near an unstable cycle, so the script does not infer a universal period merely from near repeats.

Exercises

  1. Count an exact period. Find all least-period-three states of the doubling map on \([0,1)\) and partition them into cycles.
  2. Construct a missing word. Find an irrational starting value whose doubling-map orbit has no binary prefix \(000\). Explain why its orbit stays out of \([0,1/8)\).
  3. Analyze the neutral tent. Describe every orbit of \(T_{1/2}\) after its first step, including the endpoints.
  4. Compute the first flip. At \(\mu=3.2\), find the sign of the two-cycle multiplier and explain how the sign differs from the sign of one of the factors.
  5. Locate a superstable cycle. Determine the value \(\mu>3\) for which the logistic critical point \(1/2\) belongs to a two-cycle.
  6. Study a finite-error bound. If \(f\) is globally Lipschitz with constant \(L>1\) on its invariant interval, derive a condition sufficient to keep two initially nearby trajectories within tolerance \(\eta\) for the first \(N\) iterations. Is this a guarantee of exponential growth?
Worked solutions
  1. The fixed points of \(D^3\) are \(k/7\) for \(k=0,\dots,6\). A least period dividing \(3\) is \(1\) or \(3\), and zero is the only fixed point. Doubling the nonzero residues modulo \(7\) gives the two cycles \((1,2,4)/7\) and \((3,6,5)/7\).
  2. Concatenate blocks consisting of two zeros followed by \(j\) ones for \(j=1,2,3,\ldots\). The resulting infinite binary word has unbounded runs of ones separated by exactly two zeros, so it is not eventually periodic and represents an irrational. No shift begins with three zeros. Every element of \([0,1/8)\) begins with three zeros in the canonical expansion, so the orbit misses that interval.
  3. The map equals \(x\) on \([0,1/2]\) and \(1-x\) on \([1/2,1]\). States in the first half remain fixed. States in the second half are reflected into the first half in one step and remain there. In particular \(1\mapsto0\) and \(1/2\) is fixed.
  4. \(M_2=-3.2^2+2(3.2)+4=0.16\). Both cycle points exceed \(1/2\), so both one-step factors \(\mu(1-2x_\pm)\) are negative. The displacements flip sign on each step, while after two steps the displacement has the original sign and is smaller by a factor \(0.16\) to leading order.
  5. \(f_\mu(1/2)=\mu/4\). The second step returns to \(1/2\) exactly when \(\mu^2(4-\mu)=8\), or \((\mu-2)(\mu^2-2\mu-4)=0\). The valid root above \(3\) is \(1+\sqrt5\). The root \(2\) only gives a fixed point at \(1/2\). The two-cycle multiplier vanishes at the requested root because \(f'_\mu(1/2)=0\).
  6. If \(e_n=|x_n-y_n|\), then \(e_{n+1}\le Le_n\). By induction \(e_n\le L^n e_0\). Thus \(e_0\le\eta L^{-N}\) suffices for \(0\le n\le N\). This is an upper bound. A map can have a global Lipschitz constant above one and contract along the particular trajectories, as happens near the attracting logistic fixed point for \(\mu=2.5\).
