# Langevin motion from momentum to diffusion

An experiment records the position of a particle suspended in a liquid. If we estimate diffusion by dividing mean-square displacement by elapsed time, the answer changes with the camera's sampling interval. This chapter derives the crossover. The starting model describes a velocity driven by thermal collisions and damped by friction. Integrating velocity gives nearly ballistic motion on short intervals. On long intervals the same model produces the familiar diffusive law.

## Thermal forcing needs a dissipative partner ([L28 06:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4EAPQtw1Bis&t=364s))

Consider one component $V_t$ of a particle's velocity at time $t$. The particle has mass $m>0$ and the surrounding fluid is at absolute temperature $T>0$. Ignore external forces and approximate the rapidly fluctuating net collision force as zero-mean white noise. To give the model an ordinary integral meaning, let $W_t$ be standard Brownian motion: a continuous Gaussian process starting at $W_0=0$ with independent increments of mean zero and variance equal to the increment duration. The increment $\sqrt{Q}\,dW_t$ represents the impulse over time $dt$, with $Q$ in squared impulse per time. Formally the force covariance is $Q\delta(t-s)$, where the Dirac delta is defined by integration against a test function. A physical collision force has finite correlation time. The approximation replaces it by its short-correlation limit on the observational scales of interest.

If $m\,dV_t=\sqrt Q\,dW_t$ alone, then $V_t=V_0+\sqrt Q\,W_t/m$. For fixed initial velocity, the conditional mean stays at $V_0$ and the conditional variance grows as $Qt/m^2$. In a fluid the mean motion relative to the bath should relax, and the velocity variance at thermal equilibrium is fixed. The noise-only model heats the particle without bound. Add linear drag force $-m\gamma V_t$ with relaxation rate $\gamma>0$:

$$
dV_t=-\gamma V_t\,dt+\frac{\sqrt Q}{m}\,dW_t.
$$

The rate $\gamma$ has units of inverse time. The resistance coefficient multiplying velocity in the force equation is $m\gamma$, with units of mass per time. The linear approximation presumes a regime where that drag law makes physical sense.

## Conditional relaxation fixes the noise amplitude ([L28 26:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4EAPQtw1Bis&t=1561s))

Multiplying by $e^{\gamma t}$ and integrating gives the velocity from fixed $V_0=v_0$:

$$
V_t=v_0e^{-\gamma t}+\frac{\sqrt Q}{m}\int_0^t e^{-\gamma(t-s)}\,dW_s.
$$

The noise term is a limit of sums of independent normal increments weighted by the kernel. Its mean is zero; its variance is $(Q/m^2)\int_0^t e^{-2\gamma(t-s)}\,ds$. This is the deterministic-integrand form of the Itô isometry. The resulting Gaussian transition has conditional mean $v_0e^{-\gamma t}$ and conditional variance $(Q/2m^2\gamma)(1-e^{-2\gamma t})$.

In thermal equilibrium, equipartition requires $\mathbb E[m V^2/2]=k_BT/2$ for the single quadratic degree of freedom, with $k_B$ Boltzmann's constant. Define $\sigma_v^2=k_BT/m$. Matching the long-time variance to this value fixes the forcing:

$$
Q=2m^2\gamma\sigma_v^2=2m\gamma k_BT.
$$

This fluctuation–dissipation relation is a consistency condition for this equilibrium model. Increasing drag shortens the relaxation time, while increasing the noise strength in the required proportion keeps the same equilibrium velocity width. A fixed initial velocity relaxes toward equilibrium. An independent initial draw from the centered Gaussian with variance $\sigma_v^2$ already gives a stationary velocity process. Uhlenbeck and Ornstein treated velocity and displacement moments in this Brownian model, including the small-time regime resolved by the inertia term. [Uhlenbeck and Ornstein (1930)](https://journals.aps.org/pr/abstract/10.1103/PhysRev.36.823)

## The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck kernel and correlation time ([L28 43:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4EAPQtw1Bis&t=2602s)) · [L29 05:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gW_ImQ568Po&t=349s)

This linear velocity model defines an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, with stationarity when its initial law is the equilibrium Gaussian. Its Gaussian transition kernel at positive time $t$ has mean $v_0e^{-\gamma t}$ and variance $\sigma_v^2(1-e^{-2\gamma t})$. The shrinking variance as $t\downarrow0$ gives a point mass at the specified $v_0$ in the weak limit. Future Brownian increments are independent of the past, so specifying the current velocity suffices for the conditional law of the future. The velocity density $p(v,t)$ evolves according to the Fokker–Planck equation from [Chapter 11](ch11.html):

$$
\partial_t p=\gamma\,\partial_v(vp)+\gamma\sigma_v^2\,\partial_v^2 p.
$$

Its first term contracts the law toward zero. Its second term spreads probability with diffusivity $\gamma\sigma_v^2$ in velocity units squared per time. The zero-current stationary solution has the same variance as the transition-law limit. The coincident equilibrium law is a check between the trajectory representation and the density equation.

In the stationary ensemble, multiply the conditional mean by the initial velocity and average. The covariance is

$$
C_v(t)=\mathbb E[V_0V_t]=\sigma_v^2e^{-\gamma |t|}.
$$

The normalized correlation time here is $\int_0^\infty C_v(t)/C_v(0)\,dt=1/\gamma$. Thus an idealized force with zero correlation time drives a velocity with a finite correlation time. The drag and integration filter the impulse noise. In contrast, conditioning on a deterministic initial velocity makes the covariance of the fluctuations depend on both times until the initial preparation is forgotten.

## Position alone hides the velocity state ([L29 10:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gW_ImQ568Po&t=601s))

Let $X_t$ be the particle's position on the measured axis, defined by $dX_t=V_t\,dt$. The future law given current $(X_t,V_t)$ depends only on these two numbers because the Brownian increments after $t$ are independent of the past. Position alone generally fails this test. The complete past position record up to time $t$ determines its left derivative $V_t$, since the velocity paths are continuous. At positive time $t$ a position value by itself leaves velocity uncertain. For example, even with deterministic $X_0,V_0$, the two noise integrals have kernels proportional respectively to $1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}$ and $e^{-\gamma(t-s)}$ on the same interval. These functions are linearly independent, so their Gaussian covariance matrix is positive definite by the strict Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. The conditional law of $V_t$ given $X_t$ has nonzero variance. But the small-time conditional mean displacement given the full past is $V_t\,dt+o(dt)$, which depends on more than $X_t$.

If $\rho(x,v,t)$ is the joint density, its equation is

$$
\partial_t\rho=-v\partial_x\rho+
\gamma\partial_v(v\rho)+\gamma\sigma_v^2\partial_v^2\rho.
$$

The new term is advection by the current velocity. Integrating over $v$ with vanishing boundary flux gives $\partial_t p_X=-\partial_x J$, where $p_X=\int \rho\,dv$ and $J=\int v\rho\,dv$. With inertial dynamics this conservation law does not close in $p_X$ alone. A diffusion approximation supplies a constitutive relation for $J$ when the observation time is much longer than the velocity relaxation time.

## Displacement crosses from ballistic to diffusive ([L29 20:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gW_ImQ568Po&t=1202s))

Prepare the velocity in stationarity, with mean zero. Then $\Delta X_t=X_t-X_0=\int_0^t V_s\,ds$ also has mean zero. Its square integrates a two-time covariance over the square $[0,t]^2$. Splitting the square into two triangles and changing variables to the lag $u$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[(\Delta X_t)^2]
&=2\int_0^t(t-u)C_v(u)\,du\\
&=\frac{2\sigma_v^2}{\gamma}
\left[t-\frac{1-e^{-\gamma t}}{\gamma}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

At $\gamma t\ll1$, expand the exponential to find $\mathbb E[(\Delta X_t)^2]=\sigma_v^2 t^2+O(t^3)$ at fixed $\gamma$. During this **ballistic** regime the typical displacement is that of an almost unchanged initial velocity. The leading power can differ under a different preparation. With deterministic $V_0=0$ the first noise-driven position variance has cubic onset because velocity itself must first build from random increments. For $\gamma t\gg1$, the mean square grows as $2Dt$ to leading order, with

$$
D=\frac{\sigma_v^2}{\gamma}=\frac{k_BT}{m\gamma}.
$$

The dimension of $D$ is length squared per time. Setting the measured mean-square displacement equal to $2Dt$ too early biases the estimate low. In the same model, the exact ratio $\mathbb E[(\Delta X_t)^2]/(2t)$ equals $D[1-(1-e^{-\gamma t})/(\gamma t)]$. That correction is the testable consequence of the inertial time scale.

## The diffusion equation is a long-time description ([L29 28:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gW_ImQ568Po&t=1681s))

In a diffusion model on the real line the probability density $p(x,t)$ obeys conservation $\partial_t p=-\partial_x J$. Fick's constitutive law is $J=-D\partial_xp$ for constant diffusivity $D$, leading to $\partial_t p=D\partial_x^2p$. For a point initial condition at $x_0$ the normalized solution is the Gaussian with mean $x_0$ and variance $2Dt$. More generally, assume boundary terms vanish and moments exist. Integration by parts gives $\frac{d}{dt}\int xp\,dx=0$ and $\frac{d}{dt}\int x^2p\,dx=2D\int p\,dx=2D$. Thus the variance slope follows directly from conservation and the law for the flux. Einstein's treatment derives the diffusion description from independent displacements over intervals assumed large enough to lose microscopic memory. [Einstein (1905), §§4–5](https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/gold/pdfs/teaching/old_literature/Einstein1905.pdf)

The finite-time position increments of the inertial process are correlated through velocity. The position marginal for fixed $X_0$ and stationary initial velocity is Gaussian, because it is a linear functional of the Gaussian velocity trajectory. Its actual variance is the crossover expression above. Agreement of its large-time variance with the diffusion solution explains the leading large-scale density approximation. A claim about convergence of full rescaled trajectories requires more than agreement of one-time variances.

## State-dependent noise and the correlation formula ([L29 44:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gW_ImQ568Po&t=2641s))

For a scalar state $Y_t$ with Itô equation $dY_t=f(Y_t)\,dt+g(Y_t)\,dW_t$, the test-function rule is $\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[\varphi(Y_t)]=\mathbb E[f\varphi'+g^2\varphi''/2]$ under suitable regularity and integrability. The second term comes from the variance of the Brownian increment in the second-order Taylor term. Integration by parts gives $\partial_t p=-\partial_y(fp)+\frac12\partial_y^2(g^2p)$. The placement of $g^2$ inside the derivative matters when the fluctuations depend on the state. A different stochastic convention for a state-dependent amplitude needs a corresponding drift conversion. For the constant-noise position diffusion model one sets $f=0$ and $g=\sqrt{2D}$.

The covariance argument extends to a jointly measurable velocity process with zero mean, stationarity, and finite second moments. These assumptions allow the finite-time integrals over the velocity paths and the interchange of the time integrals with expectation. For absolutely integrable $C_v$, divide the triangle formula by $2t$ and use dominated convergence on $(1-u/t)\mathbf1_{u<t}C_v(u)$. It follows that

$$
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb E[(\Delta X_t)^2]}{2t}
=\int_0^\infty C_v(u)\,du.
$$

This velocity-autocorrelation formula is a Green–Kubo relation in the transport setting. It identifies the coefficient from an equilibrium fluctuation measurement, once stationarity and convergence apply. A slowly decaying covariance can invalidate the integral and the ordinary diffusion limit. In the Langevin example the exponential integral recovers $D=\sigma_v^2/\gamma$ without any fitting. The broader response framework relates equilibrium time fluctuations to transport and susceptibility. [Kubo (1957)](https://doi.org/10.1143/JPSJ.12.570)

## Exercises

1. **Check fluctuation–dissipation.** At fixed mass and temperature, double the drag rate. What happens to the noise covariance intensity, equilibrium velocity variance, and spatial diffusivity?
2. **Compute a transient covariance.** Start at fixed $V_0=v_0$. For $t\ge s\ge0$, derive the covariance of the velocity at these two times after subtracting each conditional mean.
3. **Determine the role of preparation.** Start with $V_0=0$. Show that the leading short-time displacement variance is $2\gamma\sigma_v^2t^3/3$.
4. **Interpret a noisy camera estimate.** Let the sampling lag satisfy $\gamma t=1$. Give the exact ratio of the apparent diffusivity $\mathbb E[(\Delta X_t)^2]/(2t)$ to $D$ in the stationary-velocity model.
5. **Check a long-memory boundary.** Suppose a stationary mean-zero velocity has nonnegative covariance $C_v(u)=c/(1+u)$ for $u\ge0$, with time expressed in fixed units and $c>0$. Integrate the covariance formula and determine the leading growth of the mean-square displacement.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Worked solutions</summary>

1. Since $Q=2m\gamma k_BT$, the intensity doubles. Equipartition keeps $\sigma_v^2=k_BT/m$ fixed. The velocity correlation time and $D=\sigma_v^2/\gamma$ each halve.
2. The common noise interval is $[0,s]$. The later noise increments are independent of the earlier random part. The covariance is $2\gamma\sigma_v^2\int_0^s e^{-\gamma(t-u)}e^{-\gamma(s-u)}\,du=\sigma_v^2[e^{-\gamma(t-s)}-e^{-\gamma(t+s)}]$. The second term records the deterministic preparation and vanishes after both times are large.
3. Integrating the velocity kernel in time gives the position noise kernel $\sqrt{2\gamma\sigma_v^2}(1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)})/\gamma$ over $s\in[0,t]$. For small $t$ it is asymptotic to $\sqrt{2\gamma\sigma_v^2}(t-s)$. Squaring and integrating yields $2\gamma\sigma_v^2t^3/3$. The quadratic term present with stationary initial velocity is absent because the initial velocity is fixed at zero.
4. At $\gamma t=1$ the bracket multiplying $D$ is $1-(1-e^{-1})=e^{-1}$. A lag of one relaxation time is still too short to recover the long-time coefficient directly from the variance ratio.
5. Write $(t-u)/(1+u)=(t+1)/(1+u)-1$. The mean square is $2c[(t+1)\log(1+t)-t]$, whose leading term is $2ct\log t$. Therefore the ordinary diffusion coefficient defined by the asymptotic variance ratio is not finite in this example.

</details>
