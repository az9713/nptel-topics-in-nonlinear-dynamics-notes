# Markov models, recurrence, and diffusion

A sensor labels the position of a deterministic map as left or right. We want the expected wait for the next left label, the shape of its waiting-time distribution, and the cumulative count of returns. A two-state Markov fit gives simple answers. Its use is justified here by the geometry of the skew tent map. To see which conclusions extend to other systems, we will derive a mean return formula that needs stationarity and recurrence but requires no Markov model.

## Two-state switching has a measurable time constant ([L23 08:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OqBRWt0UqXE&t=481s))

First consider a homogeneous continuous-time model for a signal switching between values $x_1,x_2$. The rate from state 1 to state 2 is $a>0$ and the reverse rate is $b>0$. For the column probability vector $(p_1,p_2)^T$, the gain–loss rule in [Chapter 10](ch10.html) gives $\dot p_1=-ap_1+bp_2$ and $\dot p_2=-\dot p_1$. Substituting $p_2=1-p_1$ leaves one linear equation. Its stationary law is $\pi=(b,a)^T/(a+b)$ and

$$
p_1(t)=\pi_1+[p_1(0)-\pi_1]e^{-(a+b)t}.
$$

This formula yields the transition probabilities by setting $p_1(0)$ to $1$ or $0$. For state 1, the probability of remaining without any jump through time $t$ is $e^{-at}$, since in a short interval the survival probability is multiplied by $1-a\,\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$. Its mean holding time is $1/a$. One visit to state 1 plus one to state 2 averages duration $1/a+1/b$, so the long-run state-1 fraction from the law of large numbers for these alternating visits is $(1/a)/(1/a+1/b)=\pi_1$. Both holding times differ from the correlation time $1/(a+b)$.

Let $X_t$ be the signal and $\bar x=\pi_1 x_1+\pi_2 x_2$. The transition formula implies $\mathbb E[X_t-\bar x\mid X_0]=(X_0-\bar x)e^{-(a+b)t}$. Starting in $\pi$, multiply by $X_0-\bar x$ and average to obtain

$$
\mathbb E[(X_t-\bar x)(X_0-\bar x)]=
\pi_1\pi_2(x_1-x_2)^2e^{-(a+b)|t|}.
$$

For symmetric switching at rate $a=b=\lambda$, the sign difference is an eigenvector of the generator with eigenvalue $-2\lambda$. Consequently for values $\pm c$, where $c$ is the signal magnitude, the covariance is $c^2e^{-2\lambda|t|}$. The same calculation explains the exponential covariance of a stationary reset process: if independent draws from a fixed distribution are installed at Poisson rate $\lambda$ and the signal has finite variance, only the no-reset event contributes to its centered covariance.

## An exact Markov description of the skew tent map ([L23 33:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OqBRWt0UqXE&t=2006s))

For $0<r<1$, define $T(x)=x/r$ for $0\le x<r$, and $T(x)=(1-x)/(1-r)$ for $r\le x\le1$. Its left and right branches both map their interiors onto $(0,1)$. The inverse images of a set of length $\ell$ have respective lengths $r\ell$ and $(1-r)\ell$, so the uniform law is invariant. Label the two branches $L$ and $R$. Conditioning on either label with the stationary law gives a uniform point within that branch, which its linear map sends to a uniform point on the full interval.

To prove independence of the entire label sequence, consider a **cylinder**, the set of points producing a specified finite word. Recursively taking inverse images of the terminal label interval shrinks by the factor $r$ or $1-r$ at each branch. The cylinder length is the product of all the word's label probabilities. Hence the next label is independent of the full observed past. With the convention that a probability vector is a column, the one-step transition matrix is

$$
P=\begin{pmatrix}r&r\\1-r&1-r\end{pmatrix}.
$$

It has $P^n=P$ for $n\ge1$ because its columns sum to one and are identical. Equality of one-step and multistep estimates alone is weaker than proving that every conditional law given the past agrees with the one-step law. Here the cylinder argument supplies that stronger fact. Arbitrary initial densities can invalidate the independence calculation at finite times.

## Stationary flux can circulate ([L24 08:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9-Qqa2OgM48&t=481s))

A stationary law $\pi$ for a finite chain solves $P\pi=\pi$. Thus the incoming and outgoing probability fluxes at each state balance in total. **Detailed balance** means pairwise equality $P_{ij}\pi_j=P_{ji}\pi_i$ for all states $i,j$. Summing over $j$ and using the column sums proves stationarity. The fixed skew-tent partition and the two-state jump model satisfy the analogous pairwise equalities. A deterministic clockwise step around a three-state cycle gives a stationary uniform law with positive forward flux and zero backward flux. It also fails to approach its stationary law from a single initial state. Stationarity, detailed balance, and convergence concern distinct tests.

## First return and avoidance give the same law ([L24 24:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9-Qqa2OgM48&t=1446s))

Let $T$ preserve a probability measure $\mu$, let $X_0\sim\mu$, and set $X_k=T^k(X_0)$. Fix a measurable target cell $C$ with $\mu(C)>0$. Define the first return $\tau_C=\min\{n\ge1:X_n\in C\}$, using infinity if the set is empty. We condition on $X_0\in C$. The probability of return at $n$ is the integral of the indicator that the initial and final points are in $C$ and all intermediate points are outside, divided by $\mu(C)$. Membership at time one counts as a return even if the discrete sampling had no intervening outside observation.

For $n\ge0$ put $q_n=\Pr(X_0,\ldots,X_{n-1}\notin C)$, with $q_0=1$. By stationarity the event of avoiding $C$ at times $1,\ldots,n$ has probability $q_n$. Removing the histories that also avoid it at time zero leaves

$$
\Pr(\tau_C>n\mid X_0\in C)=\frac{q_n-q_{n+1}}{\mu(C)},\qquad
\Pr(\tau_C=n\mid X_0\in C)=\frac{q_{n-1}-2q_n+q_{n+1}}{\mu(C)}.
$$

Summing the tail yields $(1-\lim_n q_n)/\mu(C)$, since a positive integer-valued variable is the sum of the indicators that it exceeds each nonnegative integer. If $\mu$ is ergodic and $\mu(C)>0$, almost every trajectory visits $C$, so $q_n\downarrow0$ and the mean is $1/\mu(C)$. Without ergodicity one can keep the numerator as the measure of trajectories ever visiting $C$. The argument uses a stationary sequence and gives the discrete form of Kac's mean recurrence law. A trajectory's return count can depend strongly on the temporal resolution, especially when several successive samples occupy one cell. Balakrishnan and coauthors study the continuous-time resolution issue explicitly. [Kac (1947)](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9904-1947-08927-8) · [Balakrishnan et al. (2000)](https://journals.aps.org/pre/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevE.61.2490)

## When returns are renewal events ([L25 06:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XLHZju11YJY&t=360s))

For the stationary skew tent map and target $L$, the avoidance probabilities are $q_n=(1-r)^n$. The second difference above is $r^2(1-r)^{n-1}$, so the conditional first-return law is $r(1-r)^{n-1}$. The same result follows directly from the probability of the word containing $n-1$ right labels followed by one left. After each return the future labels are independent of the past. In this special model inter-return times are independent with the same geometric law. If the hit probability is $r=1/4$, the mean wait is four steps.

A related equation conditions on the first hit. Let $u_n=\Pr(X_n\in C\mid X_0\in C)$, with $u_0=1$, and let $f_k$ be the first-return probability at step $k$. When arrival at a return restores the same future law, $u_n=\sum_{k=1}^n f_k u_{n-k}$ for $n\ge1$. The renewal hypothesis holds for returns to a single state of a time-homogeneous Markov chain. For returns to a set of multiple states, it needs justification because the state distribution on entry can depend on the earlier path.

The skew tent count of hits in $n$ subsequent steps has a binomial law with parameters $n,r$. For fixed $k$ and $nr\to\lambda>0$ with $r\to0$, $\binom nk r^k\to\lambda^k/k!$ and $(1-r)^{n-k}\to e^{-\lambda}$. Thus the count tends to a Poisson law. The mean recurrence formula by itself gives no Poisson limit. For example, a regular cycle can give a fixed inter-return time, and an avoidance law $q_n$ proportional to $(n+1)^{-\alpha}$ at large $n$ with a smooth power-law expansion can give a first-return law of order $n^{-\alpha-2}$. This slower tail illustrates how a recurrence mean can hide the shape of the distribution.

## From jump rates to a density equation ([L25 50:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XLHZju11YJY&t=3000s))

The final part of Lecture 25 turns from discrete returns to small jumps of a continuous observable. Let $w(x,\eta)\,d\eta$ be the rate of a jump of size $\eta$ when the state is $x$. For a smooth test function $\varphi$, its mean instantaneous change at $x$ is $\int[\varphi(x+\eta)-\varphi(x)]w(x,\eta)\,d\eta$. Define the local jump moments $A(x)=\int\eta w(x,\eta)\,d\eta$ and $B(x)=\int\eta^2w(x,\eta)\,d\eta$. Taylor expansion to second order gives the approximate mean change $A\varphi'+B\varphi''/2$. For example, a sequence of small-jump models with converging first two moments and vanishing integrated Taylor remainder has this limit on smooth compactly supported tests.

Let $p(x,t)$ be the density and assume enough regularity and decay or boundary conditions to integrate by parts. Equating the two expressions for the derivative of $\int\varphi p\,dx$ gives the forward, or Fokker–Planck, equation

$$
\partial_t p=-\partial_x(Ap)+\tfrac12\partial_x^2(Bp).
$$

The first derivative transports mass, while the second spreads it. For velocity $v$, choose linear drag $A(v)=-\gamma v$ with $\gamma>0$ and constant $B(v)=2D_v$ with $D_v>0$. The stationary zero-current equation is $\gamma v p+D_v p'=0$. Integrating gives a normalized centered Gaussian with variance $D_v/\gamma$. Agreement with equilibrium temperature $T$ for a particle of mass $m$ requires $D_v/\gamma=k_BT/m$, where $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant. [Chapter 13](ch13.html) derives the same relation from the velocity trajectory and connects it to spatial diffusion. General jumps can have significant higher moments, and truncating their generator without a limiting argument need not describe the correct path law.

## Exercises

1. **Identify switching rates.** The stationary occupancy of state 1 is $3/4$ and the covariance decay rate is $4$ per unit time. Find $a,b$ and the two mean holding times.
2. **Compute a fixed return time.** Use the uniform invariant law of a deterministic cycle with $m$ states and a singleton target. Compute the first-return law and compare its mean with Kac's formula.
3. **Find hidden nonergodicity.** Half of a stationary ensemble lies in a two-state cycle and half in a second, disjoint two-state cycle, each with uniform phase. For a target comprising one state of the first cycle, compare its conditional mean return with $1/\mu(C)$.
4. **Count rare events.** Find the limiting probability of no left labels in the next $n$ observations when $r=2/n$.
5. **Derive diffusion from a symmetric jump.** Suppose the rates for jumps of size $\pm h$ are each $D/h^2$, where $D>0$. Find the first two jump moments and bound the generator remainder for a test function with bounded third derivative.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Worked solutions</summary>

1. The equations $a+b=4$ and $b/(a+b)=3/4$ give $a=1,b=3$. The first holding time averages one time unit and the second averages one third. The covariance timescale is one quarter, set by the sum of the rates.
2. Every orbit starting in the target goes around once in $m$ steps before returning. The first-return law has unit mass at $m$, so its mean is $m$. The target's invariant measure is $1/m$ and the ensemble is ergodic on that cycle.
3. The conditional return time equals two. Now $\mu(C)=1/4$, but half the ensemble never visits $C$, so $\lim_n q_n=1/2$. The general formula gives $(1-1/2)/(1/4)=2$. The reciprocal mean formula with numerator one used ergodicity or a full-measure hitting set.
4. The probability is $(1-2/n)^n$, whose logarithm tends to $-2$. Its limit $e^{-2}$ is the zero-count mass of the Poisson distribution with parameter $2$.
5. The signed contributions to $A$ cancel. The two contributions to $B$ add to $2D$. Each Taylor remainder is bounded by $\|\varphi'''\|_\infty h^3/6$ before multiplication by the rate, so their sum is bounded by $D\|\varphi'''\|_\infty h/3$. The limiting generator is $D\varphi''$, with density satisfying $\partial_t p=D\partial_x^2p$ under the stated integration conditions.
