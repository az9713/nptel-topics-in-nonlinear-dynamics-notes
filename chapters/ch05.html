Chapter 5 · Lectures 10–11

Bifurcations and limit cycles

L10 L11

A feedback-controlled device can settle at a stable output, latch into one of two competing states, or generate a self-sustained oscillation. Changing one gain can move the system between these behaviors. The distinction matters operationally. A branch that exchanges stability can be followed through the transition. A branch that disappears can force a sudden excursion. An oscillation born from an equilibrium can become the output.

An equilibrium loses its linear decision rule at a zero eigenvalue (L10 00:13)

Let \(x\in\mathbb R\) denote a dimensionless output displacement, let \(\mu\in\mathbb R\) be a held-constant control setting, and write \(\dot x=f(x,\mu)\) for a smooth equation. A bifurcation is a qualitative change in the nearby phase portrait as a parameter crosses a critical value. At an equilibrium \(x_*\), a perturbation obeys \(\dot \xi=f_x(x_*,\mu)\xi\) to first order. A negative derivative attracts and a positive derivative repels. When \(f_x=0\), we need the actual direction of the vector field. The implicit function theorem also gives the reason to look for \(f_x=0\): a simple root with \(f_x\ne0\) continues as a local smooth equilibrium branch with the same stability sign.

Zero slope can have different causes. In a rank-one linear planar system \(\dot x=a x+b y,\ \dot y=k(a x+b y)\) with \((a,b)\ne(0,0)\), the whole line \(a x+b y=0\) consists of equilibria. With \(w=a x+b y\) the exact transverse scalar equation is \(\dot w=(a+bk)w\). By contrast, \(\dot x=x^2\) has one isolated root. Its velocity is positive on both sides. Negative initial states approach \(0\) from below, and positive initial states depart. A small change to the field can split this double root. The type of split depends on the perturbation allowed by the device.

Fold and transcritical branches solve different root problems (L10 03:44)

The family \(\dot x=\mu x+x^2=x(\mu+x)\) preserves the equilibrium \(x=0\) for every \(\mu\). The other equilibrium is \(x=-\mu\). At the first branch \(f_x=\mu\) and at the second \(f_x=-\mu\). Their stability signs exchange when the branches cross. This is the transcritical bifurcation. If a second, transverse state simply decays with eigenvalue \(-1\), the two planar branches are a node and a saddle, after excluding the case of repeated eigenvalues from the distinct-root classification. The scalar mechanism describes their exchange of the unstable direction.

Now consider \(\dot x=\mu-x^2\). For \(\mu>0\) we find \(x_*=\pm\sqrt\mu\). The derivative \(-2x_*\) gives attraction on the positive branch and repulsion on the negative branch. At \(\mu=0\) they merge at one double root. For \(\mu<0\) both disappear. This is a fold, also called a saddle-node in a planar extension with a decaying transverse direction. These two families have a double root at the critical setting. The transcritical family pins a root at \(0\) while changing the local slope. The fold shifts the vector field vertically and has no such root constraint. Lecture 10, saddle-node branch. Lecture 10, transcritical terminology

The fold leaves a measurable signature after loss of both equilibria. Put \(\mu=-\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon>0\) and fix a threshold \(a>0\). A trajectory moving from \(a\) to \(-a\) passes through a region where \(\dot x=-(\varepsilon+x^2)\) is small. Its elapsed time is

\[T_\varepsilon=\int_{-a}^{a}\frac{dx}{\varepsilon+x^2} =\frac{2}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\arctan\!\left(\frac a{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right).\]

With \(a\) held fixed this tends to infinity as \(\pi/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\) when \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\). A short observation can mistake a delayed excursion for settling. This is a prediction of the near-fold model with a fixed parameter. Other effects arise when the parameter itself changes during the passage.

The generic scalar fold conditions at \((x,\mu)=(0,0)\) are \(f=f_x=0\) with \(f_{xx}\ne0\) and \(f_\mu\ne0\). To see why these are the relevant derivatives, solve \(f(x,\mu)=0\) for \(\mu=\mu(x)\) by the implicit function theorem. Then \(\mu'(0)=-f_x/f_\mu=0\) and \(\mu''(0)=-f_{xx}/f_\mu\ne0\), so the equilibrium curve has a quadratic extremum in \(\mu\). The oriented derivatives determine which way the branches turn. Transcritical and pitchfork branches typically rely on a persistent-root constraint or a symmetry for a one-parameter realization. Wiggins, equilibrium bifurcations I

A symmetric gain can create two stable outputs (L10 34:32)

If reversing the sign of a displacement reverses its rate, \(f(-x,\mu)=-f(x,\mu)\). For a smooth field near \(0\) the leading allowed nonlinearity after the linear term is then cubic. In the model \(\dot x=\mu x-x^3\), the zero branch is attracting for \(\mu<0\). For \(\mu>0\) the zero branch repels and equilibria at \(\pm\sqrt\mu\) attract: their derivative is \(\mu-3x_*^2=-2\mu\). This is a supercritical pitchfork. The sign of the initial displacement chooses the stable branch when there is no external bias. At \(\mu=0\) the cubic drives every small displacement toward zero, but at an algebraic rate instead of an exponential one.

For the subcritical pitchfork \(\dot x=\mu x+x^3\), the nonzero branches exist for \(\mu<0\) and are unstable since \(f_x=-2\mu>0\) there. They bound the local basin of the attracting zero branch. A real device may saturate outside that basin. Any predictions about distant attracting states require terms beyond the local cubic.

The supercritical model has an effective potential \(U(x)=x^4/4-\mu x^2/2\) with \(\dot x=-U'(x)\). Attracting equilibria occur at the isolated minima since the flow points downhill and \(\dot U=-[U'(x)]^2\le 0\). Add a constant bias \(h\) and use \(f(x,\mu)=\mu x-x^3+h\). A fold must have \(\mu=3x^2\) and then \(h=-2x^3\). Eliminating \(x\) gives \(27h^2=4\mu^3\) with \(\mu\ge0\). For \(\mu>0\) and \(27h^2<4\mu^3\), the cubic's two extrema have opposite signs and it has three simple real roots. On the fold curves with \(\mu>0\) there is one double root and one simple root. At the meeting point \((\mu,h)=(0,0)\) there is a triple root. The remaining parameters give one simple real root. Thus exact reflection symmetry produces a pitchfork slice of a two-parameter family. A generic biased sweep misses the meeting point of its folds. Lecture 10, unfolding discussion

A cycle is a periodic orbit that is isolated (L11 03:45)

Two output channels can instead rotate in phase space. Let \((x,y)\) denote the two displacements and put \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). For \(r>0\), the angular variable \(\theta\) is the phase modulo \(2\pi\). Work with the smooth planar model

\[\dot x=\mu x-\omega y+\sigma r^2x,\qquad \dot y=\omega x+\mu y+\sigma r^2y,\]

where \(\omega>0\) is a constant angular frequency and \(\sigma\) is \(+1\) or \(-1\). Multiplying the equations by \(x,y\) and adding cancels the rotation term. Forming \(x\dot y-y\dot x\) cancels the radial terms. The resulting equations are

\[\dot r=r(\mu+\sigma r^2),\qquad \dot\theta=\omega.\]

A positive root \(r_*\) of the radial equation gives a periodic orbit of period \(T=2\pi/\omega\). If the root is isolated, neighboring constant-radius orbits are absent and this orbit is a limit cycle. Each point on it moves even though its radius is constant. In a linear center every nonzero orbit has arbitrarily close periodic neighbors, so none qualifies as a limit cycle. Lecture 11, isolation criterion

For the positive-radius cycle, a small radial displacement \(\rho\) obeys \(\dot\rho=b\rho\) to first order, where \(b=\mu+3\sigma r_*^2=-2\mu\). A Poincaré return map takes a point on a transverse radial segment to its next intersection with the same segment. In this model the return time is the same \(T\) everywhere and the derivative of the map at the cycle is \(e^{bT}\), obtained by integrating the perturbation equation for one turn. If the derivative is less than \(1\) in absolute value, nearby trajectories approach the cycle in radius. Their final phase can depend on their initial phase. The model's linear angular velocity makes this phase freedom explicit.

An area-preserving planar flow offers a useful boundary. Suppose a regular periodic orbit has a small transverse segment and a local return map. The swept tube of trajectories between two points on the segment preserves transverse flux by the divergence theorem: the two joining sides are trajectories and carry no normal flux. Hence return preserves the measure given by the nonzero normal velocity times segment length. Its integral from a fixed point on the cycle defines a strictly monotone local coordinate. Since the return preserves the integral, it is the identity in that coordinate. The orbit is surrounded by other periodic orbits. This argument uses planar area preservation and an ordinary local return on a segment.

The nonlinear radial coefficient determines the Hopf outcome (L11 18:02)

The origin has eigenvalues \(\mu\pm i\omega\). For \(\sigma=-1\), an attracting cycle \(r_*=\sqrt\mu\) exists for \(\mu>0\). When \(\mu\) grows through zero, the origin changes from a sink to a source and the small attracting cycle emerges. This is a supercritical Hopf bifurcation. For \(\sigma=+1\), the cycle instead exists for \(\mu<0\) at \(r_*=\sqrt{-\mu}\) and repels in radius. It encloses the locally attracting origin until it shrinks into that origin at zero. Above zero the origin repels. This is the subcritical Hopf bifurcation. Despite identical linear eigenvalues, the models have different local attractors.

In the general smooth planar Hopf theorem, a conjugate pair has nonzero imaginary part and crosses the imaginary axis with nonzero derivative of its real part as the parameter changes. A nonzero first Lyapunov coefficient, the effective cubic radial coefficient after changes to normal-form coordinates, gives the leading saturation or destabilization term. When that coefficient vanishes, the sign and existence of a small cycle cannot be decided at cubic order. Results of this form apply locally. For example, if a subcritical model's polynomial approximation predicts arbitrarily large growth, it says only that the local restoring mechanism has failed. Kuznetsov, Elements of Applied Bifurcation Theory, §3.4. Lecture 11, subcritical case

Several distinct cycles can coexist (L11 31:29)

The family \(\dot r=\sin(\pi r)\), \(\dot\theta=1\) has isolated cycles at all positive integer radii. The radial derivative at radius \(j\) is \(\pi(-1)^j\). Odd radii attract and even radii repel. The attracting cycle at \(1\) has basin \(0<r<2\): between zero and one the radial velocity is positive, and between one and two it is negative. At the endpoint \(r=0\) the Cartesian field has an equilibrium. At \(r=2\) it has a separate periodic orbit. Initial conditions on those invariant sets stay there.

In the van der Pol oscillator \(\ddot x-\eta(1-x^2)\dot x+x=0\) with \(\eta>0\), the dimensionless quadratic energy \(E=(x^2+\dot x^2)/2\) obeys \(\dot E=\eta(1-x^2)\dot x^2\). Near the origin this is energy injection and at large \(|x|\) it is energy removal. This identity explains the feedback mechanism without establishing an exact cycle amplitude, period, or uniqueness. Those properties need a separate nonlinear analysis. Its limit cycle need not be a circle in \((x,\dot x)\). Lecture 11, nonlinear damping

Laboratory

The bifurcation lab uses fixed parameters and runs three checks. It compares exact near-fold passage time with direct quadrature and an ODE event. It checks the discriminant criterion for the imperfect pitchfork at two bias values, and compares the Hopf return multiplier with a Cartesian simulation. The trial perturbation is small but finite, so only agreement within a tolerance is expected. No trajectory needs to be interpreted as proof of global stability.

Exercises

  1. For the folded family \(\dot x=\mu-4x^2\), locate the equilibria and classify them. For \(\mu=-\varepsilon<0\), how does the passage-time prefactor from \(a\) to \(-a\) differ from the chapter's example as \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\)?
  2. For \(\dot x=x(\mu-2x)\), identify the bifurcation and compare the stable branch for \(\mu=\pm1\). Explain the effect of restricting the admissible domain to \(x\ge0\).
  3. For the biased pitchfork at \(\mu=3\), find the critical biases where three roots merge to one through a double root. Classify the equilibria at \(h=0\).
  4. At \(\mu=1/4\), \(\sigma=-1\), and \(\omega=2\), find the cycle radius, period, and derivative of the return map in the radial coordinate.
  5. A radial system obeys \(\dot r=r(\mu+r^2-r^4)\) with \(\dot\theta=1\). Determine the positive cycle radii and stability at \(\mu=-3/16\).
  6. In the sine-radius example, give the basin of the cycle at \(r=3\). Calculate its radial return multiplier and compare it with the one at \(r=2\).
Worked solutions
  1. For \(\mu>0\), the roots are \(\pm\sqrt\mu/2\) with derivative \(-8x\), giving attraction for the positive root. At zero they merge and for negative \(\mu\) both are absent. Substitute \(u=2x/\sqrt\varepsilon\) in \(\int_{-a}^a dx/(\varepsilon+4x^2)\). The answer is \(\varepsilon^{-1/2}\arctan(2a/\sqrt\varepsilon)\), asymptotic to \(\pi/(2\sqrt\varepsilon)\). Its prefactor is half the original because the quadratic term is steeper.
  2. The equilibria are \(0\) and \(\mu/2\), with derivatives \(\mu\) and \(-\mu\). The branches cross and exchange stability, so it is transcritical. At \(\mu=-1\) the zero branch attracts and the negative root repels. At \(\mu=1\) the positive root \(1/2\) attracts and zero repels. The half-line is forward invariant since uniqueness forbids crossing the zero solution. For negative \(\mu\) the second root lies outside the physical domain and only the zero attracting equilibrium is observed there.
  3. At a fold \(x=\pm1\) from \(\mu=3x^2\). Then \(h=-2x^3\), giving \(h=\mp2\). There is one simple root and one double root at each of these values. At \(h=0\) the roots are \(0,\pm\sqrt3\), with derivatives \(3\) at zero and \(-6\) at either nonzero root.
  4. The radius is \(1/2\) and the period is \(T=\pi\). The radial derivative of the velocity is \(-2\mu=-1/2\), so the return multiplier is \(e^{-\pi/2}\).
  5. With \(s=r^2\), the equation \(\mu+s-s^2=0\) has roots \(s=1/4,3/4\). The derivative of the radial velocity at a positive root is \(2s(1-2s)\). It is positive at \(s=1/4\), giving a repelling cycle at \(r=1/2\), and negative at \(s=3/4\), giving an attracting cycle at \(r=\sqrt3/2\). The origin attracts locally since \(\mu<0\). The inner cycle separates the two basins.
  6. Between \(2\) and \(3\) the radial velocity is positive. Between \(3\) and \(4\) it is negative. Thus \(2<r<4\) is the basin. Each turn takes \(2\pi\). The derivative of the velocity at \(3\) is \(-\pi\), giving multiplier \(e^{-2\pi^2}\). At \(2\) it is \(+\pi\), giving \(e^{2\pi^2}\) and radial repulsion.
