Planar phase portraits and invariant manifolds

L3 L4

An operator can measure two displacement channels near a balanced state. The useful question is whether a perturbation decays, spirals around the balance, or leaves along a particular route. An eigenvalue calculation predicts the directions only infinitesimally. To distinguish these outcomes at finite displacement, we will need invariant curves and information about how nearby trajectories move together.

A planar linear system has two spectral coordinates (L3 02:53)

Let \(z=(x,y)^T\in\mathbb R^2\), with \(T\) denoting transpose. Take the constant linear equation \(\dot z=Az\), where the dot denotes a derivative with respect to time and \(A\) is a real matrix. If this equation comes from a smooth nonlinear field \(F\) near an equilibrium \(z_*\) with \(F(z_*)=0\), then \(z\) represents the displacement from \(z_*\) and \(A=DF(z_*)\) is the Jacobian at the equilibrium. Write \(\tau=\operatorname{tr}A\) for the sum of the diagonal entries and \(\Delta=\det A\) for the determinant. An exponential solution \(z(t)=e^{\lambda t}v\) with \(v\ne0\) requires \(Av=\lambda v\). Evaluating the determinant of \(\lambda I-A\), with \(I\) the identity matrix, gives

\[\lambda^2-\tau\lambda+\Delta=0,\qquad \lambda_\pm=\frac{\tau\pm\sqrt{\tau^2-4\Delta}}2 .\]

Changing coordinates by an invertible linear map replaces \(A\) with a similar matrix and preserves this polynomial. The trace and determinant therefore classify the eigenvalues without choosing a basis. The eigenvectors determine how the resulting portrait is oriented in the original coordinates.

For two distinct real roots, their eigenvectors form a basis and the coordinates along those vectors obey \(\dot u=\lambda_+u\) and \(\dot v=\lambda_-v\). Integration gives \(u(t)=u_0e^{\lambda_+t}\) and \(v(t)=v_0e^{\lambda_-t}\). For \(\lambda_+>0>\lambda_-\) the equilibrium is a saddle. Along \(u=0\) the displacement tends to zero forward in time. Along \(v=0\) it tends to zero backward in time. When both roots are negative, all trajectories tend to the origin and the equilibrium is a sink of node type. Both positive roots give a source of node type. A generic orbit in a node aligns with the slower mode when approaching the equilibrium, because the ratio of the faster decaying term to the slower one tends to zero.

The repeated-root case needs more than the eigenvalues. If both roots are \(\lambda\) and there are two eigenvectors then \(A=\lambda I\), so the linear flow is radial. Otherwise \(A=\lambda I+N\) with \(N\ne0\) and \(N^2=0\). In that case \(e^{tA}=e^{\lambda t}(I+tN)\). This follows from the power series for the matrix exponential because the powers of \(N\) of degree two and above vanish. The exponential sets attraction or repulsion when \(\lambda\ne0\) and the \(tN\) term bends generic trajectories. For example, with \(\lambda=-1\) and \(N_{12}=1\) as its only nonzero entry, starting from \((0,1)\) gives \((te^{-t},e^{-t})\) as opposed to the radial trajectory \((0,e^{-t})\) for \(A=-I\). Both systems have the same \((\tau,\Delta)=(-2,1)\).

Rotation, friction, and the boundary of linear classification (L3 26:15)

When the roots are \(\alpha\pm i\omega\) with \(\omega>0\), a real linear change of coordinates puts the equation in the form \(\dot u=\alpha u-\omega v\), \(\dot v=\omega u+\alpha v\). Now \(r^2=u^2+v^2\) has derivative \(2\alpha r^2\), since the two cross terms cancel. For a nonzero trajectory the polar angle satisfies \(\dot\theta=(u\dot v-v\dot u)/r^2=\omega\). Thus a negative \(\alpha\) gives an inward focus, a positive \(\alpha\) an outward focus, and zero \(\alpha\) a family of circles in these coordinates. The corresponding equilibrium of the linear system is a center. In the original coordinate system the circles may be ellipses.

Linear spectral condition Geometry
\(\Delta<0\) Real roots of opposite sign, saddle
\(\Delta>0,\ \tau^2>4\Delta\) Distinct real roots, node with attraction for \(\tau<0\)
\(\Delta>0,\ \tau^2=4\Delta\) Repeated real root, inspect its eigenspace
\(\Delta>0,\ \tau^2<4\Delta,\ \tau\ne0\) Focus with attraction for \(\tau<0\)
\(\Delta>0,\ \tau=0\) Linear center
\(\Delta=0\) At least one zero eigenvalue

For a mechanical example let \(q\) be displacement and \(p=\dot q\) velocity, with time and displacement scaled so the undamped frequency is \(1\). A dimensionless damping coefficient \(\gamma\) gives \(\dot q=p,\ \dot p=-q-\gamma p\). Here \(\tau=-\gamma\), \(\Delta=1\), and

\[\lambda_\pm=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{\gamma^2}{4}-1}.\]

At \(\gamma=0\) all nonzero level curves of \(E=(q^2+p^2)/2\) are periodic. For \(0<\gamma<2\) the linear center becomes an attracting focus. The bracketed factor under the square root matters: the angular frequency is \(\sqrt{1-\gamma^2/4}\), and \(\gamma=2\) is the repeated-root threshold. The sign of damping controls the energy balance directly, \(\dot E=q p+p(-q-\gamma p)=-\gamma p^2\). At \(\gamma=1\) the roots are \(-1/2\pm i\sqrt3/2\). In this construction an arbitrarily small friction term removes the whole family of periodic orbits. Isolated attracting periodic orbits behave differently, as Chapter 5 develops. Lecture 3, perturbing the center

An equilibrium is hyperbolic when no eigenvalue of its Jacobian has zero real part. Near a hyperbolic equilibrium of a continuously differentiable autonomous field, the Hartman–Grobman theorem gives a local topological equivalence to the linear portrait. The equivalence concerns orbit geometry and should not be used to infer distances or global basins. For a nonlinear field with a center spectrum the theorem is silent. For example, add radial terms \(\pm r^2(u,v)\) to \(\dot u=-v,\ \dot v=u\). Both systems have the same linear rotation. Their radii satisfy \(\dot r=\pm r^3\), so one tends inward and the other eventually moves out of every sufficiently small disk. This example also shows why a linear center needs a separate nonlinear invariant or argument. Wiggins, equilibrium linearization

A saddle's straight eigenspaces become invariant curves (L4 13:47)

For an equilibrium \(z_*\), the stable set consists of initial states whose forward solutions exist for all positive time and approach \(z_*\). The unstable set uses backward solutions approaching \(z_*\) as \(t\to-\infty\). For the local version keep the respective forward or backward orbit in a specified neighborhood. At a hyperbolic saddle for a smooth planar vector field, the stable and unstable manifold theorem gives one-dimensional local curves tangent at \(z_*\) to the contracting and expanding eigenspaces. Local curves can be continued by the flow. Information about their distant branches needs the nonlinear field.

Consider the original dimensionless model \(\dot x=-x,\ \dot y=y+x^2\). Its Jacobian at the origin is \(\operatorname{diag}(-1,1)\), so the contracting linear direction is the \(x\) axis. For a graph \(y=h(x)\) to be invariant, the tangent condition is \(h'(x)\dot x=\dot y\). Taking \(h(x)=a x^2\) gives \(-2a x^2=(a+1)x^2\), hence \(a=-1/3\). On introducing \(w=y+x^2/3\), we get \(\dot w=w\) exactly. The solutions are

\[x(t)=x_0e^{-t},\qquad y(t)=(y_0+x_0^2/3)e^t-\frac{x_0^2}{3}e^{-2t}.\]

The stable set is the entire graph \(y=-x^2/3\). The unstable set is the entire line \(x=0\), because \(x(t)\) would diverge as \(t\to-\infty\) if \(x_0\ne0\). From \((1,0)\) the trajectory leaves the equilibrium's vicinity rather than approaching it. The initial point \((1,-1/3)\) has the same displacement along the contracting eigendirection, and approaches the origin. This is the distinction an operator needs to recover from finite-displacement data.

An eigenvalue with zero real part contributes to the center subspace, the sum of the corresponding generalized eigenspaces interpreted over the reals. Local center manifolds, when constructed for a sufficiently smooth system, are invariant surfaces tangent to this subspace. They need not be unique. For illustration, in \(\dot x=-x^3,\ \dot y=-y\) an invariant graph satisfies \(x^3h'(x)=h(x)\) for \(x\ne0\). Each

\[h(x)=C\exp[-1/(2x^2)]\quad(x\ne0),\qquad h(0)=0\]

is smooth at zero with all derivatives zero there, so all have the same tangent. The reduced equation \(\dot x=-x^3\) is common to these graphs and attracts nearby \(x\) values at an algebraic rate. Wiggins, stable and unstable manifolds. Wiggins, center manifold theory. Lecture 4, center subspace

Divergence measures local area change, even when the axes shear (L4 33:15)

Let \(\phi_t(z_0)\) be the flow map taking an initial point \(z_0\) to its position at time \(t\). Assume \(F\) is continuously differentiable and solutions exist on the interval under consideration. With \(B(t)=D\phi_t(z_0)\), differentiation of \(\partial_t\phi_t=F(\phi_t)\) with respect to \(z_0\) gives \(\dot B=DF(\phi_t)B\), \(B(0)=I\). Infinitesimal tangent vectors transform by this same matrix; it can shear a square into a parallelogram. Its signed area factor \(J=\det B\) remains positive because \(B\) is invertible by uniqueness and local time reversal. Jacobi's determinant formula then yields

\[\frac{d}{dt}\log J=\operatorname{tr}(B^{-1}\dot B) =\operatorname{tr}DF(\phi_t)=\nabla\cdot F(\phi_t),\qquad J(t)=\exp\left[\int_0^t\nabla\cdot F(\phi_s)\,ds\right].\]

The trace of \(DF\) is the divergence, the sum of its diagonal entries. Where this vanishes identically, the area factor is \(1\) and initial sets retain their area by the change-of-variables formula. Differential stretching can still be large. The exact saddle above has divergence \(-1+1=0\), regardless of its curved stable set. Stable and unstable directions compensate in the area balance. Strictly negative divergence on a forward-invariant domain reduces area of sets within that domain. In the damped oscillator it is the constant \(-\gamma\), and an initial disk of area \(S\) has area \(Se^{-\gamma t}\) at time \(t\).

Cycles in a predator–prey model preserve a weighted area (L4 44:48)

Let \(x,y>0\) denote dimensionless prey and predator abundance. In the symmetric Lotka–Volterra model both intrinsic rates and both interaction coefficients equal \(1\) in the chosen scales, giving \(\dot x=x(1-y)\) and \(\dot y=y(x-1)\). The axes are invariant by uniqueness. In the positive quadrant the sole equilibrium is \((1,1)\) and its linearization has imaginary eigenvalues. The Euclidean divergence is \(x-y\), which only vanishes on the diagonal. The linearization alone leaves both the nearby portrait and the long-time area behavior unresolved.

Set \(q=\log y\) and \(p=\log x\), where \(\log\) denotes the natural logarithm. The logarithmic coordinates satisfy \(\dot q=e^p-1\), \(\dot p=1-e^q\). Defining

\[H(q,p)=e^q-q-1+e^p-p-1\]

gives \(\dot q=\partial_p H\), \(\dot p=-\partial_q H\). Hence \(\dot H=0\) by the chain rule. Each term diverges at both ends of its real domain and has second derivative \(e^q\) or \(e^p\), always positive. The origin is the unique minimum. Along each ray from the origin the function grows strictly from zero to infinity, so each positive level is a regular closed curve surrounding the origin. The field is tangent to the curve and nonzero, giving periodic motion with finite period on each such curve. The conserved quantity in the original variables is \(x-\log x-1+y-\log y-1\).

The area measure \(dq\,dp\) corresponds to \(dx\,dy/(xy)\), since the absolute Jacobian of the logarithmic transformation is \(1/(xy)\). The divergence vanishes in the logarithmic coordinates. The net Euclidean area factor for an infinitesimal patch along one trajectory returns to \(1\) after its period, because

\[\frac{d}{dt}\log(xy)=(1-y)+(x-1)=x-y.\]

The area factor through time is therefore \(x(t)y(t)/(x_0y_0)\). Finite neighborhoods generally include orbits with different periods, so the one-orbit calculation applies to the differential area factor. The exact weighted conservation law holds for finite sets as well. The original predator model considered ecological interactions under idealized assumptions about resources and rates. The logarithmic construction explains persistence within this symmetric model. Lecture 4, logarithmic coordinates

Exercises

  1. Compute \(\tau\), \(\Delta\), and the critical damping value for \(\dot q=p\), \(\dot p=-4q-\gamma p\). At \(\gamma=3\) give the eigenvalues and the area factor at time \(t=1\).
  2. Give two matrices with characteristic polynomial \((\lambda+3)^2\) but different orbit geometry. Start each flow from \((0,1)\). Evaluate both at \(t=1\) and explain the difference.
  3. In \(\dot x=-2x,\ \dot y=3y+x^2\), determine the stable graph \(y=h(x)\) of the form \(h(x)=a x^2\). Verify it by exhibiting a scalar coordinate that grows at rate \(3\).
  4. Change the sign of \(\dot x\) in the center-manifold example \(\dot x=-x^3,\ \dot y=-y\). Solve the same invariance equation for a graph through the origin and determine whether its previously exhibited family remains possible.
  5. For the symmetric predator–prey model, prove \(\frac1T\int_0^T x(t)\,dt=\frac1T\int_0^T y(t)\,dt=1\) on a positive periodic orbit of period \(T\). Explain what this does and does not say about its amplitude.
  6. For \(\dot x=x,\ \dot y=-x-y\), compute the area factor and give a displacement whose length grows. Explain why this poses no contradiction.
Worked solutions
  1. The state matrix is \(\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-4&-\gamma\end{pmatrix}\), so \(\tau=-\gamma\) and \(\Delta=4\). The discriminant is \(\gamma^2-16\) and the positive damping threshold is \(4\). At \(\gamma=3\) the roots are \((-3\pm i\sqrt7)/2\) and the area factor is \(e^{-3}\).
  2. Use \(A=-3I\) and \(B=-3I+N\) with \(N_{12}=1\) and all other entries zero. The first trajectory is \((0,e^{-3t})\). In the second, \(N^2=0\) and \((I+tN)(0,1)^T=(t,1)^T\), giving \((e^{-3},e^{-3})\) at time \(1\). The Jordan term shears the trajectory while the decay exponent is shared.
  3. Equating \(h'(x)(-2x)\) to \(3h(x)+x^2\) yields \(-4a=3a+1\), so \(a=-1/7\). For \(w=y+x^2/7\) we have \(\dot w=3y+x^2-4x^2/7=3w\). Therefore every \(w_0\ne0\) moves away in the \(w\) coordinate while \(w_0=0\) permits convergence to the origin.
  4. Now \(x^3h'=-h\), so the nonzero solutions on either side are multiples of \(\exp[1/(2x^2)]\). They diverge as \(x\to0\), ruling out all but the zero continuous graph. The linear center subspace is still the \(x\) axis. The sign of the nonlinear center equation determines which neighboring trajectories stay near the origin in forward time.
  5. Integrate \(\frac d{dt}\log y=x-1\) over one period and use \(y(T)=y(0)\), so \(\int_0^T x\,dt=T\). Likewise integrate \(\frac d{dt}\log x=1-y\) to get \(\int_0^T y\,dt=T\). This is compatible with every positive level of \(H\), which gives a different excursion and need not have the same period.
  6. The matrix has trace zero, so the area factor is \(1\). The vector \((1,-1/2)^T\) is an eigenvector for eigenvalue \(1\) and grows by \(e^t\). The second eigenvalue is \(-1\), so contraction along its eigendirection compensates for expansion in the determinant.
